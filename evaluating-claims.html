Evaluating Supplement Claims

Updated Feb 2026 · 10 min read

The supplement industry spends billions on marketing. Here's how to separate science from sales pitch.

Red Flags in Marketing

🚩 "Clinically Proven"

This phrase is meaningless. What study? How many people? Who funded it? Published where? Without details, it's marketing fluff.

🚩 Miracle Claims

"Cures cancer," "reverses aging," "eliminates disease." If it sounds too good to be true, it is. Real medicine rarely uses such language.

🚩 "Doctors Don't Want You to Know"

Conspiracy framing is a red flag. If something worked, doctors would use it—they want to help patients.

🚩 Testimonials Instead of Studies

"I lost 30 pounds!" Testimonials are cherry-picked, unverifiable, and often paid. They're not evidence.

🚩 "Ancient Wisdom" or "Secret Formula"

Appeals to antiquity or secrecy aren't evidence. Many ancient remedies don't work or are harmful.

🚩 Proprietary Blends

When a label hides exact amounts behind "proprietary blend," you can't know what you're getting. Companies hide doses to cut costs.

Reliable Information Sources

✅ NIH Office of Dietary Supplements

ods.od.nih.gov — Fact sheets on supplements, evidence summaries, safety info. Government-funded, no conflicts.

✅ Examine.com

Independent, evidence-based supplement analysis. No ads, no industry funding. Summarizes research clearly.

✅ ConsumerLab

Tests supplements for quality, purity, and label accuracy. Subscription required but valuable for verifying brands.

✅ Cochrane Reviews

cochranelibrary.com — Gold standard systematic reviews of health interventions, including supplements.

✅ PubMed

pubmed.gov — Search actual research studies. Look for randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews.

Questions to Ask

  • What specific health problem am I trying to address?
  • Is there peer-reviewed research supporting this supplement for this use?
  • How many studies? How large? In humans?
  • Who funded the research?
  • What dose was studied vs. what's in this product?
  • Are there safety concerns or drug interactions?
  • Could I address this through diet instead?
  • What does my doctor think?

Understanding Study Quality

Not all studies are equal:

  • Randomized controlled trials (RCTs): Gold standard. Participants randomly assigned to supplement or placebo.
  • Systematic reviews/meta-analyses: Combine multiple RCTs. Most reliable overall evidence.
  • Observational studies: Show associations, not causation. Useful but can be misleading.
  • Animal/cell studies: Interesting but don't predict human effects. "Works in mice" means almost nothing.
  • Single studies: May not replicate. Weight of evidence from multiple studies matters more.
