How Supplements Are Regulated in the US: DSHEA, FDA, and What It Means for You

February 2026

If you have ever wondered why a supplement can claim to "support immune health" but not "prevent colds," or why a product can contain less of an ingredient than the label states and still be legal, the answer traces back to a single piece of legislation: the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994, commonly known as DSHEA.

Understanding DSHEA is essential for anyone who takes supplements. It explains why the supplement market operates so differently from the pharmaceutical market, and why the burden of protecting yourself falls largely on you.

What DSHEA Changed

Before 1994, dietary supplements were regulated primarily as food additives, which required pre-market safety approval from the FDA. The supplement industry argued this was too restrictive and lobbied heavily for a new framework. DSHEA was the result.

The law redefined supplements as a distinct regulatory category -- neither food nor drug -- and established several principles that remain in effect today:

  • No pre-market approval required. Unlike drugs, which must demonstrate safety and efficacy through clinical trials before reaching consumers, supplements can be sold without any prior FDA review. The manufacturer is responsible for ensuring that the product is safe and that label claims are truthful.
  • The burden of proof is reversed. For drugs, the manufacturer must prove safety before selling. For supplements, the FDA must prove a product is unsafe after it is already on the market before it can be removed.
  • Structure/function claims are permitted. Supplements can make claims about how a nutrient affects body structure or function ("supports bone health") but cannot claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent disease ("prevents osteoporosis"). The distinction is subtle and often exploited.
  • New Dietary Ingredient notification. Ingredients not sold in the US before October 1994 require the manufacturer to notify the FDA 75 days before marketing, providing evidence of reasonable safety. In practice, enforcement of this provision has been inconsistent.

How This Plays Out in Practice

The consequences of DSHEA's framework are substantial and well-documented:

Label Accuracy Is Not Guaranteed

Independent testing organizations regularly find products that do not contain what their labels claim. A 2013 study published in BMC Medicine used DNA barcoding to test 44 herbal supplements from 12 companies and found that 59% contained plant species not listed on the label. One-third of the products contained contaminants or fillers with no herbal content at all.

The New York Attorney General's office conducted its own investigation in 2015, testing store-brand herbal supplements from four major retailers. Four out of five products did not contain any of the herbs listed on the label. GNC, Target, Walmart, and Walgreens all received cease-and-desist letters.

Contamination and Adulteration

The FDA's own data tells a troubling story. Between 2007 and 2021, the agency identified over 1,000 supplements adulterated with undeclared pharmaceutical ingredients. Weight loss products frequently contain sibutramine (withdrawn from the market for cardiovascular risk) or undisclosed stimulants. Sexual enhancement supplements often contain sildenafil (Viagra) or analogs. Sports supplements have been found to contain anabolic steroids.

A 2018 study in JAMA Network Open analyzed 776 dietary supplements that had received FDA warnings for adulteration. Nearly all were in three categories: sexual enhancement (45%), weight loss (41%), and muscle building (12%). Disturbingly, the same products remained on the market years after FDA warnings in many cases.

Adverse Event Reporting Is Passive

Since 2007, supplement manufacturers are required to report serious adverse events to the FDA. However, this system is passive -- it depends on consumers or healthcare providers filing reports, and most adverse events go unreported. The FDA estimates it receives reports on only 1% of actual supplement-related adverse events.

Despite this underreporting, supplements are associated with an estimated 23,000 emergency room visits per year in the United States, according to a 2015 study in the New England Journal of Medicine. About 28% involved young adults using weight loss or energy products.

What the FDA Can and Does Do

It would be inaccurate to say supplements are completely unregulated. The FDA retains several authorities:

  • Good Manufacturing Practices (GMPs): Since 2010, supplement manufacturers must follow GMP regulations covering production processes, quality control, record-keeping, and handling. The FDA inspects facilities, though the pace of inspections is slow relative to the number of manufacturers.
  • Warning letters and enforcement: The FDA issues warning letters to companies making illegal disease claims or selling adulterated products. It can seize products and seek injunctions. In practice, enforcement resources are limited compared to the scale of the market.
  • Mandatory recall authority: Under the Food Safety Modernization Act (2011), the FDA gained mandatory recall authority for dietary supplements, though voluntary recalls remain far more common.
  • New Dietary Ingredient notifications: The FDA reviews safety notifications for new ingredients, though the process is less rigorous than drug approval.

The fundamental limitation is one of scale. There are an estimated 80,000+ supplement products on the US market, produced by thousands of manufacturers. The FDA's resources for supplement oversight are a fraction of what is needed for comprehensive enforcement.

How Consumers Can Protect Themselves

Given the regulatory gaps, informed consumers need to take several steps that would be unnecessary in a pharmaceutical context:

  1. Look for third-party testing. USP Verified, NSF Certified for Sport, and ConsumerLab are independent organizations that verify supplement contents, purity, and potency. These certifications are voluntary and cost the manufacturer money, which means the companies that pursue them are generally more committed to quality.
  2. Be skeptical of claims. If a supplement promises to cure disease, enhance performance dramatically, or produce results that sound too good, it is likely either lying or illegally adulterated. Legitimate supplements make modest, specific claims.
  3. Check for recalls and warnings. The FDA maintains a database of supplement recalls and warning letters. The website FDA Safety Recalls is a useful resource.
  4. Avoid proprietary blends. Products that list ingredients under a "proprietary blend" without individual amounts are hiding how much of each ingredient you are getting. In most cases, expensive ingredients are underdosed while cheap fillers make up the bulk.
  5. Report adverse events. If you experience a negative reaction to a supplement, report it to the FDA through the MedWatch program. This contributes to the safety data that the FDA needs to take enforcement action.

High-Risk Categories

Three categories of supplements carry the highest risk of adulteration with undeclared pharmaceuticals: weight loss products, sexual enhancement products, and bodybuilding/muscle-building products. If you use products in these categories and have not verified them through third-party testing, you may be unknowingly taking pharmaceutical drugs at uncontrolled doses.

The Bottom Line

DSHEA created a regulatory framework that gives supplement manufacturers far more latitude than drug manufacturers, and the FDA far less power to act proactively. The system depends heavily on manufacturer self-regulation and consumer vigilance. Third-party testing is the single most important tool available to consumers for verifying supplement quality. Until the regulatory framework changes, "buyer beware" remains the operative principle.
