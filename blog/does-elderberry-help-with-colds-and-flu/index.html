Does Elderberry Help With Colds and Flu?

October 8, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Maybe a little, and the evidence is thin. Two small, older trials reported that elderberry syrup eased flu symptoms about 4 days sooner than placebo. A 2020 trial of 87 emergency room patients with lab-confirmed flu found no benefit at all. A 2021 systematic review concluded that elderberry "may" shorten colds and flu, and called that evidence uncertain. Elderberry does not replace a flu vaccine or prescription antivirals.

What did the early flu trials find?

The positive reputation of black elderberry (Sambucus nigra) rests mostly on two trials from the same research group.

  • The 1995 trial. Researchers gave an elderberry extract or placebo to people in an Israeli kibbutz during a 1993 influenza B outbreak. They reported that 93.3% of the elderberry group improved within 2 days. In the placebo group, 91.7% improved within 6 days.
  • The 2004 trial. Sixty adults in Norway, aged 18 to 54, had flu-like symptoms for 48 hours or less. They took elderberry syrup or placebo syrup for 5 days. The authors reported that symptoms were relieved "on average 4 days earlier" with elderberry. The elderberry group also used less rescue medication.

Both results look large. The 2004 authors still wrote that their findings "need to be confirmed in a larger study."

How small and how old is the evidence?

Very small, and mostly more than 20 years old. A 2014 review searched six databases through November 2013. It found three clinical trials of elderberry. All three concluded that elderberry worked against influenza, but only 77 people in total received the treatment.

A 2019 meta-analysis pooled randomized trials. It covered 180 participants and reported a large average effect on upper respiratory symptoms. Its own abstract notes that "there are no large-scale studies." A pooled result from 180 people cannot settle the question.

Small trials tend to overstate benefits. The same pattern appeared with zinc, where early lozenge trials looked stronger than later reviews. Our article on whether zinc shortens a cold covers that history.

What did the newer trials find?

Two newer trials give a mixed picture.

The air traveler trial (2016). An Australian team randomized 312 economy class passengers on long overseas flights to an elderberry extract or placebo. Elderberry did not clearly prevent colds. There were 17 cold episodes in the placebo group and 12 in the elderberry group, and the difference was not significant (p = 0.4).

Among the travelers who did catch a cold, the elderberry group fared better. The placebo group logged 117 total days of cold illness, and the elderberry group logged 57 (p = 0.02). Symptom scores were also lower with elderberry. Only 29 cold episodes occurred in the whole trial, so these figures come from few people.

The emergency room trial (2020). This trial confirmed every flu case with a lab test. Cleveland Clinic researchers enrolled 87 patients aged 5 and older in three emergency rooms. Each patient had a positive PCR test for influenza and fewer than 48 hours of symptoms. They took elderberry extract or placebo for 5 days.

The result was null:

  • Time until all symptoms were mild or gone: 4.9 days with placebo and 5.3 days with elderberry (p = 0.57).
  • Time to complete recovery: 8.7 days with placebo and 8.6 days with elderberry (p = 0.87).

The authors wrote, "We found no evidence that elderberry benefits the duration or severity of influenza." They said the trial had more than 90% power to detect a 2-day benefit. A post hoc analysis even suggested worse outcomes with elderberry alone than with placebo alone.

What do systematic reviews conclude?

The most careful summary is a 2021 systematic review in BMC Complementary Medicine and Therapies. The reviewers screened 1,187 records and included five randomized trials. They graded the certainty of each finding. Their conclusions were cautious:

  • Elderberry "may not reduce the risk of developing the common cold."
  • It "may reduce the duration and severity of colds, but the evidence is uncertain."
  • It "may reduce the duration of influenza but the evidence is uncertain."

The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) uses similar language. It says "some preliminary research suggests" that elderberry may relieve symptoms of flu, colds, or other upper respiratory infections. NCCIH also says only a small number of studies exist. It states that there is no good evidence to support elderberry for COVID-19.

Is elderberry safe?

The 2021 review said elderberry "may be a safe option" for viral respiratory illness. Three safety questions come up often.

Raw and unripe berries. NCCIH warns that raw or unripe elderberries, and the leaves and stems, contain cyanide-producing substances. These can cause nausea, vomiting, and severe diarrhea. Cooking eliminates the toxin.

A CDC report describes what can go wrong with homemade juice. In August 1983, a group in Monterey County, California, pressed wild elderberries together with leaves and branches. Eleven people developed nausea and vomiting within 15 minutes of drinking it, and eight were flown to a hospital. All recovered quickly.

Pregnancy and breastfeeding. The data gap is real. The 2014 review found no human studies and no animal studies of elderberry safety in pregnancy. Its authors advised health care staff not to recommend elderberry to pregnant women for respiratory infections. NCCIH says little is known about safety during pregnancy or breastfeeding.

The "cytokine storm" concern. During the COVID-19 pandemic, some people worried that elderberry could overstimulate the immune system. The 2021 systematic review looked for this specifically. It found no studies linking elderberry to clinical inflammatory outcomes. The reviewers concluded that "there is no evidence that it overstimulates the immune system." They also said the evidence on harms is uncertain.

Herbal products can also interact with medicines. See our page on supplements that interact with prescription drugs, and ask a pharmacist about your own list.

Does the FDA approve elderberry for colds or flu?

No. Elderberry products are sold as dietary supplements. NCCIH explains that dietary supplements are not approved by the FDA before they are sold. Our explainer on how supplements are regulated gives the details.

The FDA acts when sellers cross that line. In a warning letter dated January 30, 2023, the agency cited an elderberry tincture marketed with the claim "Recommended to help prevent and ease cold and flu symptoms." Because of claims like that one, the FDA called the product an unapproved new drug. It was "particularly concerned" that the tincture was marketed for children and infants.

NCCIH adds that the FDA and the Federal Trade Commission took action against companies that marketed elderberry with unsubstantiated COVID-19 claims.

Can elderberry replace a flu shot or antivirals?

No. The CDC recommends a yearly flu vaccine as "the first and most important action in reducing your risk of flu." It advises vaccination for everyone 6 months and older. No elderberry trial shows that it prevents influenza.

For people who are already sick, the CDC flu treatment page recommends early antiviral treatment for those at higher risk of serious complications. Examples include people with asthma, diabetes, or heart disease. Antivirals work best when started within two days after symptoms begin. They require a prescription.

That timing matters. Someone at higher risk who waits to see whether a syrup works may miss the treatment window. If you are unsure whether your illness is a cold or the flu, the symptom guides at symptom.md can help you describe it to a clinician.

The bottom line

Elderberry might ease cold or flu symptoms somewhat, but nobody can say so with confidence. The positive flu trials were small and old. The 2020 trial in lab-confirmed flu found no benefit. Reviewers call the overall evidence uncertain.

Raw berries, leaves, and stems can cause poisoning, and pregnancy safety data do not exist. Elderberry is not an FDA-approved treatment, and it is not a substitute for a flu vaccine or for antivirals in people at higher risk.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does elderberry prevent you from catching a cold?

Probably not. In a 2016 trial of 312 air travelers, the elderberry group had 12 cold episodes and the placebo group had 17, and the difference was not statistically significant. A 2021 systematic review concluded that elderberry may not reduce the risk of developing a cold.

How many days faster do you recover from the flu with elderberry?

The trials disagree. A 2004 trial of 60 adults reported symptom relief about 4 days earlier with elderberry syrup. A 2020 trial of 87 patients with lab-confirmed flu found no difference: 5.3 days with elderberry and 4.9 days with placebo.

Can you eat raw elderberries?

NCCIH warns against it. Raw or unripe berries, leaves, and stems contain cyanide-producing substances that can cause nausea, vomiting, and severe diarrhea. Cooking eliminates the toxin.

Is elderberry syrup safe during pregnancy?

Nobody knows. A 2014 review found no human or animal studies of elderberry safety in pregnancy, and its authors advised against recommending it to pregnant women. Ask your obstetric clinician before using any herbal product.

Can elderberry cause a cytokine storm?

A 2021 systematic review looked for this and found no studies linking elderberry to clinical inflammatory harm. The reviewers concluded there is no evidence that elderberry overstimulates the immune system. They also said the evidence on harms is uncertain.
