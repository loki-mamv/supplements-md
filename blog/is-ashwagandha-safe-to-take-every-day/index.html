Is Ashwagandha Safe to Take Every Day?

September 7, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Probably, for a few months. The NIH says ashwagandha appears well tolerated for up to about 3 months of use, and that its long-term safety is not known. Daily use past that point runs ahead of the evidence. Rare but real liver injury, thyroid hormone changes, and a pregnancy warning mean some people should not take it at all.

What is ashwagandha?

Ashwagandha is the root of Withania somnifera, an evergreen shrub grown in Asia, Africa, and southern Europe. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements (ODS) notes the species name somnifera is Latin for sleep-inducing. Ayurvedic medicine has used the root for centuries as an adaptogen, a substance thought to help the body resist stress.

The main active compounds are steroidal lactones called withanolides, plus alkaloids. Root and leaf have different chemistry. Most supplements use root extract, some use root plus leaf, and withanolide content varies by product. A 300 mg capsule of one extract is not the same as 300 mg of another.

What do trials show, and how long did people take it?

Most people take ashwagandha for stress, anxiety, or sleep. The trials behind those uses are small and short. A 2024 meta-analysis in Explore pooled 9 randomized trials with 558 patients. Ashwagandha lowered Perceived Stress Scale scores by 4.72 points and Hamilton Anxiety Scale scores by 2.19 points versus placebo. Serum cortisol fell too. Four of the nine trials reported mild to moderate adverse events. The authors wrote that more information is required to determine its safety with long-term administration.

A 2022 meta-analysis in Phytotherapy Research covered 12 trials with 1,002 participants aged 25 to 48. It found large reductions in anxiety and stress, but rated the certainty of evidence as low. A 2025 review in BJPsych Open of 15 studies and 873 patients found the cortisol and stress effects were clearest at 8 weeks.

For sleep, a 2021 meta-analysis in PLoS One found a small but significant effect (standardized mean difference -0.59). ODS summarizes it: five trials in India, 372 adults, 6 to 12 weeks, 250 to 600 mg per day of root extract or 120 mg per day of a root and leaf extract.

Notice the pattern. Trial doses ranged from about 120 mg to 1,250 mg of extract per day, and trials ran 30 to 90 days. Nobody has run a randomized trial of daily use for a year. If stress is your reason, anxiety.md covers treatments with a longer safety record.

Can ashwagandha hurt the liver?

Rarely, yes. LiverTox, the NIH liver injury database, gives ashwagandha a likelihood score of B, meaning a likely cause of clinically apparent liver injury. Injury typically appears 2 to 12 weeks after starting, with jaundice and itching. Most cases were mild to moderate and resolved within 1 to 4 months of stopping. LiverTox also records rare fatal cases and liver transplants, mostly in people with pre-existing liver disease.

The case reports keep coming. ODS describes five people aged 21 to 62 who took 450 to 1,350 mg per day for 1 week to 4 months and developed liver injury. A 2023 series in Hepatology Communications reported 23 patients with ashwagandha-related liver injury at Indian centers between January 2019 and December 2022. Eight took single-ingredient products. Three arrived with acute-on-chronic liver failure and all three died. Chemical analysis of the retrieved products found only natural phytochemicals, with no adulteration. A 2023 review in Pharmaceuticals added two cases: a 36-year-old man who took 450 mg three times a day for 6 months, and a 30-year-old woman who developed itching after 45 days on 450 mg capsules. Both improved after stopping. Trials have not reported liver enzyme elevations, so this is uncommon. But rare events need large numbers to show up, and the trials were small.

Does ashwagandha change thyroid and other hormones?

It can raise thyroid hormone levels. ODS cites a trial of 50 people with subclinical hypothyroidism in which 300 mg of root extract twice a day for 8 weeks lowered TSH and raised T3 and T4 compared with placebo. In a separate bipolar disorder study, three men on 500 mg per day for 8 weeks had T4 increases of 7%, 12%, and 24%. ODS also lists three case reports of thyrotoxicosis in women aged 32 to 73, all resolved after stopping. One published case involved a 73-year-old woman who used ashwagandha for two years to self-treat hypothyroidism and arrived with supraventricular tachycardia and very low TSH.

For a healthy thyroid, a small nudge upward may cause nothing. For someone on levothyroxine or with Graves' disease, it can tip hormone levels into the overactive range. Learn what those lab values mean at thyroid.md. ODS adds that ashwagandha may raise testosterone.

Who should not take ashwagandha at all?

Based on NCCIH and ODS, the list is:

  • Pregnant or breastfeeding women. The root has a traditional history of use to induce abortion. No study has set a level below which that effect does not occur. NCCIH says to avoid it in pregnancy and while breastfeeding.
  • People with liver disease. LiverTox says avoid it with cirrhosis or advanced chronic liver disease. All three deaths in the Indian series were in people with pre-existing liver disease.
  • People with thyroid or autoimmune disorders. NCCIH lists both.
  • Anyone scheduled for surgery. NCCIH advises against use around surgery.
  • Men with hormone-sensitive prostate cancer, per ODS.

Drug interactions add another layer. NCCIH and ODS list diabetes medications, blood pressure medications, immunosuppressants, sedatives, anticonvulsants, and thyroid hormone. Our guide on whether you can take too many supplements covers how stacking effects build up.

Why did Denmark ban it, and what are other countries doing?

In May 2020 the DTU National Food Institute delivered a risk assessment to the Danish Veterinary and Food Administration. It found that animal and some human data pointed to effects on thyroid and sex hormones, noted the traditional abortifacient use, and concluded that no safe intake level for the root or root extract could be set. Denmark banned ashwagandha in food supplements in 2023.

Other agencies followed with warnings. A 2025 regulatory review in Phytotherapy Research reports that Sweden's food agency said in 2022 the Danish assessment could be applied there. The Netherlands' RIVM report (2024) advised consumers not to use ashwagandha, especially during pregnancy. RIVM noted that physicians had reported liver injury and thyroid and cortisol changes at doses similar to products sold in Dutch shops. France's ANSES warned in 2024 against use in pregnancy, breastfeeding, and endocrine disorders, per ODS. Finland has not restricted it. EU agency heads recommended an Article 8 review in 2024, which could lead to an EU-wide restriction.

The American Herbal Pharmacopoeia disputes the abortifacient claim, and ODS notes there is no clinical evidence of abortions from root extract. Regulators chose caution anyway.

Is the product in the bottle what the label says?

In the United States, ashwagandha is a dietary supplement under DSHEA. The FDA does not approve it before sale. ODS points out that trials used different extracts with different withanolide content, so a study result may not apply to the product you buy.

LiverTox adds that many implicated products were multi-ingredient blends, and mislabeling or adulteration is always possible. In several cases the product was tested and contained only ashwagandha, so the herb itself can cause injury. Read how supplement regulation works for what the label does and does not guarantee, and see which supplements have stronger evidence if you want alternatives.

The bottom line

Ashwagandha has been tolerated in trials lasting up to about 3 months, at doses of roughly 120 to 1,250 mg of extract per day. That is the extent of the safety evidence. Daily use for longer has never been tested, and the NIH says long-term safety is unknown. Rare liver injury, higher thyroid hormone levels, and a pregnancy warning are documented. If you are healthy, not pregnant, not on interacting medication, and use it for a defined period, the known risk is low. If you plan to take it every day indefinitely, you are the experiment. Tell your clinician, and stop at the first sign of jaundice, itching, a racing heart, or unexplained weight loss.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long can you stay on ashwagandha before the evidence runs out?

About 3 months. Both the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements and NCCIH say ashwagandha appears well tolerated for up to roughly 3 months. Neither agency has safety data for use over many months or years. Trials in the published meta-analyses ran 30 to 90 days.

What are the early signs of ashwagandha liver injury?

LiverTox lists jaundice (yellow skin or eyes), itching, nausea, tiredness, and dark urine, usually starting 2 to 12 weeks after the first dose. Stop the product and see a clinician if these appear. Most reported cases resolved within 1 to 4 months after stopping.

Can I take ashwagandha with levothyroxine?

Talk to your prescriber first. A small 8-week trial found ashwagandha lowered TSH and raised T3 and T4 in people with subclinical hypothyroidism. Adding it to thyroid medication could push hormone levels too high. Case reports describe thyrotoxicosis in people who combined the two or used ashwagandha alone.

Does taking a break, or cycling, make daily ashwagandha safer?

No study has tested cycling schedules, so there is no evidence either way. The safety limit of about 3 months comes from trial length, not from a study of on-and-off use. If you keep using it, tell your clinician so liver and thyroid labs can be checked if symptoms appear.

Is ashwagandha tea or powder safer than capsules?

There is no evidence that one form is safer. The Dutch RIVM report noted that no research exists on ashwagandha tea and applied its supplement conclusions to tea as well. Root powder, root extract, and root-plus-leaf extract differ in withanolide content, so the same gram amount can deliver different doses.
