Vitamin D: A Complete Guide to Benefits, Dosing, and Testing

February 2026

Vitamin D occupies a strange position in nutrition science. It is simultaneously one of the most studied micronutrients and one of the most poorly understood by the general public. Walk through any pharmacy and you will find shelves of vitamin D supplements in doses ranging from 400 IU to 50,000 IU. Social media influencers recommend megadoses for everything from depression to cancer prevention. Meanwhile, the clinical evidence tells a more measured story.

This guide covers what we actually know about vitamin D, who should take it, how much, and where the science falls short.

What Vitamin D Does in the Body

Vitamin D is a fat-soluble secosteroid that functions more like a hormone than a traditional vitamin. When ultraviolet B radiation hits the skin, it converts 7-dehydrocholesterol into cholecalciferol (vitamin D3). This precursor then undergoes two hydroxylation steps: first in the liver to produce 25-hydroxyvitamin D (calcidiol), and then in the kidneys to produce 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol), the biologically active form.

Calcitriol regulates calcium and phosphorus absorption in the gut, making it essential for bone mineralization. Without adequate vitamin D, the body absorbs only about 10-15% of dietary calcium. With sufficient levels, that figure rises to 30-40%. This is the foundation of vitamin D's best-established role: maintaining skeletal health.

Beyond bone metabolism, vitamin D receptors have been identified in most human tissues, including immune cells, cardiac muscle, and brain tissue. This has fueled research into potential roles in immune function, cardiovascular disease, cancer, and neurological health. The results, however, have been more complicated than the early observational data suggested.

Deficiency: Who Is Actually at Risk

Vitamin D deficiency, typically defined as a serum 25(OH)D level below 20 ng/mL (50 nmol/L), is genuinely common. The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data indicate that roughly 35% of US adults have levels below this threshold. Insufficiency (20-30 ng/mL) is even more prevalent.

Certain populations face substantially higher risk:

  • People living at high latitudes (above 37th parallel) who cannot synthesize adequate vitamin D from sunlight during winter months
  • Individuals with dark skin, as melanin reduces UVB-mediated synthesis by up to 99%
  • Older adults, whose skin produces about 75% less vitamin D than younger adults given the same sun exposure
  • People with obesity (BMI above 30), since vitamin D is sequestered in adipose tissue and less bioavailable
  • Those with malabsorption conditions such as celiac disease, Crohn's disease, or a history of gastric bypass surgery
  • People who consistently cover their skin or use high-SPF sunscreen, which blocks UVB penetration

If you fall into one of these groups, testing is reasonable. If you are a healthy young adult living in a sunny climate who spends time outdoors, blanket supplementation is harder to justify without a blood test.

D2 vs. D3: Does It Matter?

Vitamin D supplements come in two forms: ergocalciferol (D2) and cholecalciferol (D3). D2 is derived from fungi and yeast, while D3 comes from animal sources (lanolin, fish liver oil) or lichen-based vegan options.

The clinical difference is meaningful. A 2012 meta-analysis published in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition found that D3 is approximately 87% more potent than D2 at raising serum 25(OH)D levels, and produces two to three times greater storage of the vitamin. D3 also has a longer half-life in circulation. For these reasons, most clinical guidelines now prefer D3 for supplementation, though D2 remains available by prescription in high-dose formulations.

How Much Should You Take?

This is where things get contentious. The Institute of Medicine (now the National Academy of Medicine) set the Recommended Dietary Allowance at 600 IU daily for adults up to age 70, and 800 IU for those over 70. The Tolerable Upper Intake Level is 4,000 IU per day.

The Endocrine Society, on the other hand, has suggested that adults may need 1,500-2,000 IU daily to maintain serum levels above 30 ng/mL, with obese individuals potentially requiring two to three times that amount.

The pragmatic approach: get tested, supplement to correct a documented deficiency, and retest after 8-12 weeks to confirm your levels have reached the target range. Doses of 1,000-2,000 IU daily are well within the safety margin for most adults and are a reasonable starting point if testing is not immediately available.

What the Evidence Supports (and What It Doesn't)

Strong Evidence

Bone health and fracture prevention: In individuals with documented deficiency, vitamin D supplementation (combined with calcium) reduces fracture risk in older adults. A Cochrane review found that vitamin D with calcium reduced hip fractures by about 16% in institutionalized elderly populations. Vitamin D alone, without calcium, showed less consistent benefit.

Falls prevention in the elderly: Several meta-analyses, including one published in BMJ, have found that vitamin D supplementation (700-1,000 IU daily) reduces fall risk in older adults by approximately 19%. The mechanism likely involves improved muscle function and neuromuscular coordination.

Mixed or Insufficient Evidence

Cancer prevention: The VITAL trial, a large randomized controlled trial of over 25,000 participants, found no significant reduction in overall cancer incidence with vitamin D supplementation (2,000 IU daily). However, a secondary analysis did show a possible reduction in cancer mortality among those who developed cancer during the trial. This requires further study.

Cardiovascular disease: Despite promising observational data linking low vitamin D to heart disease, interventional trials have been largely disappointing. The VITAL trial showed no cardiovascular benefit. A 2019 meta-analysis in JAMA Cardiology reached the same conclusion.

Depression and cognitive function: Some observational studies associate low vitamin D with depression, but randomized trials have not consistently shown that supplementation improves mood in people without deficiency. There may be a role in individuals with clinically low levels, but the evidence does not support vitamin D as a treatment for depression.

Immune function and respiratory infections: A 2017 BMJ meta-analysis of 25 randomized controlled trials found that vitamin D supplementation reduced the risk of acute respiratory infections, with the strongest effect in those with baseline levels below 10 ng/mL. The effect was modest in those with adequate levels.

A Note on Toxicity

Vitamin D toxicity is rare but real. It occurs almost exclusively from supplementation, not from sun exposure or food. Chronic intake above 10,000 IU daily can lead to hypercalcemia, kidney stones, and soft tissue calcification. Cases have been reported with doses of 50,000 IU daily taken for months. Stick to recommended ranges and monitor levels if taking doses above 4,000 IU.

Testing: The 25(OH)D Blood Test

The standard test is serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D, which reflects total body stores. There is no consensus on the optimal level, which contributes to confusion:

  • Below 12 ng/mL: Deficient (risk of rickets in children, osteomalacia in adults)
  • 12-20 ng/mL: Inadequate for most people
  • 20-50 ng/mL: Adequate for the vast majority (IOM position)
  • 30-50 ng/mL: Preferred by the Endocrine Society
  • Above 50 ng/mL: Not shown to provide additional benefit; may increase risk

Testing is typically covered by insurance when ordered with a clinical indication (fatigue, bone pain, history of fracture, malabsorption). Without a clear indication, out-of-pocket cost ranges from $25-75 at most labs.

The Bottom Line

Vitamin D supplementation makes clear sense for people with documented deficiency and those in high-risk groups. For the general population with adequate sun exposure and a varied diet, the benefit of supplementation is uncertain. Get tested if you have risk factors. If you supplement, 1,000-2,000 IU of D3 daily is a safe and reasonable dose. Skip the megadoses unless your physician is monitoring your levels.
