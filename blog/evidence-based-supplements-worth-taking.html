Evidence-Based Supplements Actually Worth Taking

February 2026

The supplement industry thrives on vagueness. "Supports immune health." "Promotes wellness." "Clinically studied ingredients." These phrases fill labels and advertisements, creating an impression of scientific backing where little may exist. For consumers trying to make informed decisions, the noise-to-signal ratio is abysmal.

This article takes a different approach. Instead of reviewing every supplement on the market, it identifies the small number that have strong clinical evidence for specific populations and conditions. If a supplement is not listed here, it does not necessarily mean it is worthless -- it means the evidence is either too weak, too preliminary, or too condition-specific to make a general recommendation.

Vitamin D

Roughly 35% of US adults have serum 25(OH)D levels below the deficiency threshold of 20 ng/mL. The groups at highest risk include people living in northern latitudes, older adults, individuals with dark skin, those with obesity, and anyone with limited sun exposure. For these populations, supplementation with 1,000-2,000 IU of vitamin D3 daily is well-supported.

The evidence for bone health and falls prevention in the elderly is solid. The evidence for cancer prevention, cardiovascular disease, and depression is either mixed or negative in interventional trials, despite promising observational data. Supplement for documented deficiency or clear risk factors, not as a general-purpose health booster.

Folic Acid

This is one of the clearest success stories in supplement science. Folic acid supplementation before conception and during early pregnancy reduces the risk of neural tube defects (spina bifida, anencephaly) by 50-70%. The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends 400-800 mcg daily for all women capable of becoming pregnant.

Because about half of pregnancies are unplanned, and neural tube closure occurs in the first 28 days (often before a woman knows she is pregnant), this recommendation applies broadly to women of reproductive age, not just those actively trying to conceive.

Vitamin B12

B12 is found almost exclusively in animal products, making deficiency a near-certainty for strict vegans who do not supplement. Beyond diet, B12 absorption decreases with age due to reduced gastric acid production. An estimated 10-30% of adults over 65 have impaired B12 absorption.

Medications that further reduce absorption include metformin (used for diabetes), proton pump inhibitors, and H2 blockers. The consequences of untreated B12 deficiency are serious: megaloblastic anemia and irreversible neurological damage including peripheral neuropathy and cognitive decline.

Oral supplementation at 500-1,000 mcg daily is effective even in people with reduced absorption, because a small percentage is absorbed through passive diffusion independent of intrinsic factor.

Iron

Iron deserves special emphasis because it is one of the few supplements that is clearly beneficial when needed and clearly harmful when not. Iron-deficiency anemia should be diagnosed through blood testing (serum ferritin, CBC) before supplementation begins. Symptoms are nonspecific -- fatigue, weakness, pallor -- and overlap with dozens of other conditions.

Supplementing iron without confirmed deficiency risks iron overload, which causes oxidative damage to the liver, heart, and other organs. This is particularly concerning for people with undiagnosed hemochromatosis, a genetic condition affecting roughly 1 in 200 people of Northern European descent. Never take iron supplements without blood work.

The Iron Rule

Do not supplement iron unless you have a documented deficiency confirmed by blood testing. Iron is one of the few common supplements where excess intake carries clear, dose-dependent risk of organ damage. "Feeling tired" is not sufficient justification for iron supplementation.

Omega-3 Fatty Acids

The strongest evidence for omega-3 supplementation is triglyceride reduction. At doses of 2-4 grams per day, EPA and DHA lower triglycerides by 15-30%. This effect is robust enough to support FDA-approved prescription formulations.

For general cardiovascular prevention in healthy people, standard-dose fish oil (1 gram combined EPA+DHA) has not shown benefit in recent large trials (VITAL, ASCEND). If you eat two servings of fatty fish per week, you are likely getting adequate omega-3s without supplementation. If you do not eat fish, an algae-based supplement providing 500 mg of combined EPA+DHA is a reasonable option.

Calcium

Calcium supplementation is most clearly supported for postmenopausal women and elderly adults with low dietary calcium intake (below 700-800 mg/day). Combined with vitamin D, calcium supplementation reduces fracture risk in these populations. A 2016 meta-analysis in the BMJ found a 15% reduction in total fractures.

However, calcium supplementation is not without controversy. Some observational studies have raised concerns about cardiovascular risk, though the data is inconsistent. The current consensus is that supplemental calcium up to 1,000-1,200 mg daily (from diet plus supplements combined) is safe for most people, but exceeding this amount is not recommended.

Prenatal Multivitamins

Pregnancy increases nutritional demands across multiple nutrients simultaneously: folic acid, iron, calcium, DHA, iodine, and others. A prenatal multivitamin is the most practical way to address these increased needs, particularly because many nutrient demands peak during the first trimester when dietary intake may be compromised by nausea.

Not all prenatal formulations are equal. Look for one containing at least 400 mcg folic acid, 27 mg iron, 200-300 mg DHA, 150 mcg iodine, and 1,000 IU vitamin D. Third-party tested products (USP verified) are preferable.

Magnesium

Nearly half of US adults fall short of the magnesium RDA through diet alone. The clinical significance of this shortfall is debated, but evidence supports modest benefits for blood pressure reduction, migraine prevention, and blood sugar regulation. Magnesium citrate or glycinate at 200-400 mg daily is a reasonable supplement for people whose diets are low in magnesium-rich foods (nuts, seeds, leafy greens, whole grains).

What About Multivitamins?

The evidence for general-purpose multivitamins in healthy, well-nourished adults is weak. The largest trial to date, the Physicians' Health Study II (14,641 male physicians), found a small reduction in total cancer incidence (8%) but no effect on cardiovascular disease, cognitive decline, or mortality. The US Preventive Services Task Force found insufficient evidence to recommend multivitamins for disease prevention in healthy adults.

Multivitamins may have a role as nutritional insurance for people with poor dietary patterns, absorption issues, or restricted diets. But they are not a substitute for eating well, and they do not compensate for an otherwise unhealthy lifestyle.

Popular Supplements That Lack Strong Evidence

For the sake of completeness, here are widely-used supplements where the evidence does not support routine use in healthy adults:

  • Vitamin C for cold prevention: Routine supplementation does not reduce cold incidence in the general population. A Cochrane review found it may shorten cold duration by about 8% in adults -- roughly half a day.
  • Turmeric/curcumin: Promising in vitro and animal data, but poor bioavailability and inconsistent clinical trial results. A 2022 editorial in Nature titled "The Dark Side of Curcumin" highlighted concerns about unreliable preclinical data.
  • Collagen peptides: Some evidence for modest improvement in skin elasticity and joint pain, but studies are generally small, short, and often industry-funded. Not a high-confidence recommendation.
  • Elderberry: A few small trials suggest it may reduce cold/flu duration, but the studies are too small and low-quality to draw firm conclusions.
  • Biotin for hair and nails: No evidence of benefit in people without biotin deficiency, which is rare. However, supplemental biotin can interfere with lab tests for troponin and thyroid function, potentially causing misdiagnosis.
  • Ashwagandha: Some evidence for modest cortisol and anxiety reduction in small trials, but not enough to make a clinical recommendation. Long-term safety data is limited.

The Bottom Line

The list of supplements with strong evidence for the general population is short: vitamin D for those at risk of deficiency, folic acid for women of reproductive age, B12 for vegans and older adults, and omega-3s for people who do not eat fish. Everything else is either condition-specific (iron for diagnosed anemia, calcium for postmenopausal bone health) or lacking sufficient evidence for a blanket recommendation. Test before you supplement. Match the product to the evidence, not the marketing.
