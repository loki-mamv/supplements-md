Are Gummy Vitamins as Good as Pills?

September 17, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida
Table of Contents

The short answer: For the vitamins they contain, gummies can be absorbed about as well as pills. Small trials of vitamin D and vitamin C gummies found equal or better blood levels than tablets. The bigger gaps are elsewhere: most adult gummy multivitamins leave out iron and nearly all calcium, each serving adds sugar, and gummies look like candy to young children. If you can swallow pills, a tablet usually gives you more nutrients per dose. If you cannot, a gummy is a reasonable choice.

Do gummy vitamins absorb as well as tablets?

The few head-to-head studies say yes, for the nutrients tested. In a 2019 crossover study in Nutrients, 31 healthy adults took a single 20,000 IU dose of vitamin D3 as gummies or as tablets, two weeks apart. Blood vitamin D3 over 48 hours was about twice as high after the gummies (area under the curve 1,474 vs 774 ng·h/mL), and peak levels were 47.3 vs 23.4 ng/mL (Wagner et al., Nutrients 2019).

A 2020 randomized crossover trial in 30 adults compared a 1,000 mg vitamin C gummy with a 1,000 mg caplet. Total vitamin C absorption in plasma, white blood cells, and urine did not differ in a meaningful way, and the authors judged the two bioequivalent (Evans et al., J Am Coll Nutr 2020).

Two limits apply. Both studies used one large dose, not months of daily use. And both had industry ties: the gummy maker funded part of the vitamin D study, and employees of the same company co-wrote the vitamin C trial. No trial we found compares gummy and tablet multivitamins on health outcomes. Whether a daily multivitamin helps at all is a separate question, covered in our review of whether multivitamins do anything.

Do gummies contain what the label says?

Not always, and gummies are harder to make consistent than tablets. FDA does not approve supplements before they reach stores. Makers are responsible for the safety and labeling of their own products (FDA). Under federal labeling rules, added vitamins and minerals must be present at least at the amount declared on the label (21 CFR 101.9(g)).

Vitamins break down over time, so makers add extra, called an overage. One vitafusion MultiVites label says so directly: the product is "manufactured with higher active ingredient levels than the indicated amount per serving" to keep potency through expiration (NIH Dietary Supplement Label Database). That means a fresh bottle may hold more than the label, and an old one may hold less.

The vitamin D study above shows the drift. An outside lab measured about 2,092 IU per gummy before the first study and about 1,751 IU at its end, roughly 16% less in under two months. Tablet content also varied between batches (Wagner et al.).

For vitamin gummies in general, we found no large, independent, peer-reviewed label audit. The clearest gummy data come from melatonin. A 2023 JAMA analysis found that 22 of 25 melatonin gummy products were mislabeled, with 74% to 347% of the labeled amount (NCCIH; Cohen et al., JAMA 2023). An earlier Canadian study, cited by CDC, found the most variation in chewable forms (MMWR 2022). Our guide to supplement regulation explains what USP and NSF seals check.

What do gummy multivitamins leave out?

Iron and calcium are the usual gaps. Compare two adult products from the same brand in the NIH label database:

  • Centrum Adults tablet: 26 vitamins and minerals, including 18 mg iron (100% of the Daily Value), 200 mg calcium, 50 mg magnesium, and 55 mcg selenium (DSLD).
  • Centrum MultiGummies Adults: 14 vitamins and minerals, with no iron, calcium, magnesium, selenium, or vitamin K. Folate is 40 mcg DFE (10% of the Daily Value) (DSLD).

Where a gummy does include calcium, the amount is small. Centrum Women MultiGummies list 100 mg, or 8% of the Daily Value (DSLD). Poison Control notes that children's gummy or chewable multivitamins "might not contain any iron at all," or only a very low amount (Poison Control). If you take a multivitamin for iron, or for folic acid before pregnancy, read the Supplement Facts panel. The gummy version may not cover it.

How much sugar do gummy vitamins add?

Usually 2 to 3 grams of added sugar per serving. Glucose syrup and sugar are the first two ingredients in both vitafusion MultiVites and Centrum MultiGummies. A two-gummy serving lists 3 g and 2 g of added sugars (DSLD; DSLD). FDA sets the Daily Value for added sugars at 50 grams (FDA). So one serving is small, but it adds up if you take several gummy products a day.

Teeth are the larger concern. MedlinePlus states that sticky foods are more harmful than non-sticky foods because they stay on the teeth. It advises eating chewy, sticky foods with a meal rather than alone as a snack (MedlinePlus). Taking a gummy with breakfast and brushing afterward follows that advice.

Are gummy vitamins a risk for young children?

Yes, because children treat them as candy. A regional poison center study found 1,143 calls about gummy products in children over 2015 to 2017. Median age was 3, 74% involved vitamins, and 7% of children had symptoms (Crawford et al., Clin Toxicol 2021). Most vitamin gummy exposures are mild, but not all. One toddler given calcium and vitamin D gummies several times a day for weeks reached a blood calcium of 20.7 mg/dL and needed intensive care (Doyle et al., Pediatr Emerg Care 2021).

Melatonin shows how fast the problem grew. Poison center reports of pediatric melatonin ingestions rose 530%, from 8,337 in 2012 to 52,563 in 2021 (MMWR 2022). From 2019 to 2022, an estimated 10,930 emergency visits involved children 5 and under, and 47.3% of those involved gummies (MMWR 2024).

Iron is the most dangerous ingredient. Federal rules require iron supplements in solid form to warn that accidental overdose of iron-containing products "is a leading cause of fatal poisoning in children under 6" (21 CFR 101.17(e)). Store every supplement, gummy or not, up high and out of sight. Our article on taking too many supplements covers upper limits for adults.

Who should choose gummies over pills?

Gummies make most sense for people who will not or cannot take pills. That group is large. In a survey of 1,051 primary care patients, 37.4% reported trouble swallowing tablets or capsules, and 9.4% of those said they skipped doses (Schiele et al., Eur J Clin Pharmacol 2013). In older adults, choking or pills stuck in the throat caused 37.6% of emergency visits for supplement side effects, and vitamins or minerals were involved in 83.1% of those (Geller et al., NEJM 2015).

Reasonable reasons to pick a gummy:

  • You have trouble swallowing pills and have skipped doses because of it.
  • You need a single nutrient that gummies deliver well, such as vitamin D.
  • You have no need for iron or calcium from the product.

Reasons to pick a tablet or capsule instead: you need iron, calcium, or a full mineral range, you are limiting sugar, or young children live in your home. Chewable tablets, liquids, and powders are other options. For broader habits that matter more than pill form, see thrive.md.

The bottom line

Gummy vitamins are not a weaker version of pills for the nutrients they actually contain. Vitamin D and vitamin C gummies matched or beat tablets in small absorption studies. But a typical adult gummy multivitamin lists about half as many nutrients as a tablet from the same brand, with no iron and little or no calcium. It adds 2 to 3 grams of sugar and tempts small children. Choose gummies if swallowing pills is the barrier. Otherwise, a third-party-tested tablet gives you more per dose.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do gummy vitamins sometimes hold more than the label says?

Vitamins break down during storage, so makers add extra at production so the product still meets its label at expiration. Federal labeling rules require added vitamins to be present at least at the declared amount. A fresh bottle may hold more than listed, and an older one may hold less.

Can adults take children's gummy vitamins?

They can, but children's gummies are made for children's needs. Poison Control notes that children's gummy multivitamins may contain no iron or only a very low amount. Compare the Supplement Facts panels before you switch.

What should I do if my child eats a bottle of gummy vitamins?

Call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 right away, even if your child seems fine. Have the bottle ready so you can read the ingredients and amounts. Products with iron or melatonin need especially quick attention.

Is vitamin D in gummy form absorbed well?

In a 2019 crossover study of 31 adults, a single vitamin D3 dose in gummies produced about twice the blood vitamin D3 exposure of the same dose in tablets. The study used one large dose and was partly funded by the gummy maker. Whether your dose is enough depends on your blood level and your doctor's advice.

Do gummy vitamins count toward daily sugar limits?

Yes. Many adult gummy multivitamins list 2 to 3 grams of added sugars per serving on the Supplement Facts panel. FDA sets the Daily Value for added sugars at 50 grams, so one serving is small, but several gummy products a day add up.
