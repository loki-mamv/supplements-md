Does Zinc Shorten a Cold?

October 5, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Maybe, by a couple of days, but the evidence is weak. The 2024 Cochrane review found that zinc taken as a cold treatment may shorten a cold by about 2.4 days, and it rated that finding low certainty. Zinc does not seem to prevent colds, and it probably causes more side effects than placebo. Zinc nasal sprays and gels are a separate matter: the FDA linked them to lasting loss of smell.

What does the best evidence say about zinc and cold duration?

The most complete summary is the Cochrane review published in May 2024. It pooled 34 randomized trials with 8,526 participants. Nineteen trials tested zinc as a treatment and 15 tested it for prevention. Twenty-two trials enrolled adults and 12 enrolled children.

For treatment, the reviewers wrote that there "may be a reduction in the mean duration of the cold." The pooled difference was 2.37 days shorter with zinc, across 8 trials and 972 participants. The 95% confidence interval ran from 0.53 to 4.21 days, so the true effect could be half a day or four days.

The reviewers graded this as low-certainty evidence. The trials disagreed with each other a great deal (the heterogeneity statistic was 97%), and most trials had an unclear or high risk of bias in at least one area.

Other outcomes looked weaker:

  • Still sick at the end of follow-up: uncertain, very low-certainty evidence.
  • Overall symptom severity: uncertain, very low-certainty evidence.
  • Serious side effects: no treatment trial reported on them.

So the honest reading is this: zinc might shorten a cold, and nobody can yet say by how much or for whom.

Does zinc prevent colds in the first place?

Probably not. In the same Cochrane review, people who took zinc to prevent colds had about the same risk of catching one as people on placebo. The risk ratio was 0.93, with a confidence interval of 0.85 to 1.01, from 9 trials and 1,449 participants. The reviewers rated that as low-certainty evidence of little or no effect.

Preventive zinc also did not clearly shorten the colds that people did catch. The reviewers found "probably little or no difference" in cold duration, with moderate-certainty evidence.

This differs from some older summaries. The MedlinePlus page on zinc in the diet still says zinc taken for at least 5 months may reduce the risk of catching a cold. The 2024 Cochrane review is newer and does not support that claim with confidence.

Why do older studies sound more positive?

Earlier meta-analyses looked at narrower sets of trials, and several found larger effects.

  • A 2012 meta-analysis in CMAJ pooled 17 trials with 2,121 participants. Zinc shortened cold symptoms by 1.65 days on average. The effect was 2.63 days in adults, and no significant effect was seen in children.
  • A 2016 individual patient data meta-analysis pooled three zinc acetate lozenge trials with 199 patients. It estimated colds were 2.73 days shorter, against an average cold of 7 days in those trials.
  • A 2017 meta-analysis of seven lozenge trials with 575 participants found colds were 33% shorter with zinc lozenges at doses above 75 mg per day.

These numbers are real, but they come with limits. The CMAJ authors reported high heterogeneity (95%) and said large, high-quality trials were needed before firm recommendations. The lozenge analyses rest on small trials selected by dose and formulation.

The history of this topic is also messy. An earlier Cochrane review of zinc for the common cold was withdrawn in 2015 after concerns about how its data were calculated and analyzed. Which forms and doses of zinc were studied?

Most of the research is on lozenges. In the Cochrane review, 17 of 34 trials used lozenges, and zinc gluconate was the most common form. The reviewers reported zinc gluconate doses between 45 and 276 mg per day, given for 4.5 to 21 days. Other trials used tablets, syrups, capsules, dissolved powders, and nasal products.

The 2017 lozenge meta-analysis compared zinc acetate with zinc gluconate and found no significant difference between them. Five of its trials used 80 to 92 mg per day and two used 192 to 207 mg per day. The higher doses did not work better. The author concluded there is no evidence that doses over 100 mg per day add benefit.

Timing also follows the trial designs. The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) states that oral zinc lozenges may reduce cold duration "when started within 24 hours and taken for a time period of less than 2 weeks."

These are the doses researchers tested. They are not a recommendation, and they sit above the daily upper limit described below. A clinician or pharmacist can tell you whether short-term zinc makes sense for you.

What are the side effects, and what is the upper limit?

Side effects are the most certain finding in the research. The Cochrane review found that zinc used for cold treatment probably raises the risk of non-serious adverse events by about a third (risk ratio 1.34, moderate-certainty evidence, 16 trials).

The CMAJ meta-analysis named the most common ones. Bad taste was 65% more likely with zinc than with placebo, and nausea was 64% more likely.

The National Academies set the Tolerable Upper Intake Level for adults at 40 mg of zinc per day. That figure counts zinc from food, water, and supplements together. It is based on the way excess zinc lowers copper status. The National Academies note that the limit does not apply to people taking zinc under medical supervision.

For comparison, the Recommended Dietary Allowance is 11 mg per day for men and 8 mg per day for women. Many of the cold trials used about twice the upper limit or more, for a short time.

Longer use carries more risk. NCCIH warns that long-term zinc, especially in high doses, can cause copper deficiency. MedlinePlus says large amounts may cause diarrhea, abdominal cramps, and vomiting, usually within 3 to 10 hours. Zinc may also interact with medicines, including antibiotics and penicillamine. Are zinc nasal sprays and gels safe?

No. Zinc that goes into the nose is a different product from a lozenge, and it carries a specific risk.

In June 2009 the FDA issued a public health advisory on three Zicam intranasal zinc products. The agency had received more than 130 reports of anosmia (loss of smell) linked to those products since 1999. Many people reported that the loss came with the first dose. The FDA said the loss "may be long-lasting or even permanent" and told consumers to stop using the products and throw them away.

The FDA also noted that the advisory did not concern oral zinc tablets and lozenges. NCCIH states plainly that intranasal zinc "should not be used."

Loss of smell has practical costs. The FDA pointed out that people may fail to smell smoke, a gas leak, or spoiled food. If you lose your sense of smell during or after a cold, tell a clinician. The symptom guides at symptom.md can help you describe what you are noticing.

The bottom line

Zinc may shorten a cold by roughly two days, but the best current review calls that low-certainty evidence. It probably does not prevent colds. The clearest finding is that zinc causes more side effects than placebo, mainly bad taste and nausea.

Many lozenge trials used doses above the 40 mg adult upper limit, for days rather than weeks. Zinc nasal sprays and gels should be avoided because of the risk of lasting smell loss. A cold usually clears without treatment, so skipping zinc is a reasonable choice too.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many days shorter is a cold with zinc?

The 2024 Cochrane review estimated 2.37 days shorter on average when zinc was used as a treatment. The plausible range ran from about half a day to about four days. The reviewers rated this as low-certainty evidence, so the real effect may differ.

Is it safe to take zinc lozenges every day in winter?

The research does not support daily zinc for prevention. The 2024 Cochrane review found little or no reduction in the risk of catching a cold. Long-term high-dose zinc can cause copper deficiency, and the adult upper limit from the National Academies is 40 mg per day from all sources.

Do zinc lozenges work for children with colds?

The evidence in children is weaker than in adults. A 2012 meta-analysis in CMAJ found no significant effect on cold duration in children. Ask a pediatrician before giving a child any zinc product.

Is zinc acetate better than zinc gluconate for a cold?

A 2017 meta-analysis of seven lozenge trials found no significant difference between the two forms. MedlinePlus also says it is not known whether one form of zinc is better than the others.

Can zinc lozenges cause loss of smell?

The 2009 FDA advisory on loss of smell covered zinc products used inside the nose. The FDA said the advisory did not concern oral zinc tablets and lozenges. The common side effects of lozenges in trials were bad taste and nausea.
