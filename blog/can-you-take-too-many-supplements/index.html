Can You Take Too Many Supplements at Once?

August 25, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: yes. The risk rarely comes from one product. It comes from the same nutrient appearing in a multivitamin, a greens powder, a pre-workout, and a fortified food at the same time. The NIH publishes a Tolerable Upper Intake Level for each nutrient, and a stack of normal-looking products can cross it. Supplements send an estimated 23,000 people to US emergency departments each year, and herbal and dietary products now cause about 20% of registered drug-induced liver injury cases.

What is a Tolerable Upper Intake Level?

The Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) is the highest daily intake unlikely to cause harm in healthy people. It counts food, fortified food, and supplements together. It is a ceiling, not a target. These are the adult ULs for the six nutrients that cause the most trouble, as published by the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements:

  • Preformed vitamin A: 3,000 mcg RAE per day (ODS vitamin A). Beta-carotene does not count toward it.
  • Vitamin D: 100 mcg (4,000 IU) per day (ODS vitamin D).
  • Vitamin B6: 100 mg per day (ODS vitamin B6). In 2023 the European Food Safety Authority set a far lower limit of 12 mg, based on nerve damage reports.
  • Iron: 45 mg per day (ODS iron).
  • Zinc: 40 mg per day (ODS zinc).
  • Selenium: 400 mcg per day (ODS selenium). The 2023 EFSA limit is 255 mcg.

How do doses add up across products?

Most people who exceed a UL never buy a high-dose product. They buy four normal ones: a multivitamin with 100% of the Daily Value for zinc, B6, and vitamin A; a greens powder with another round of B vitamins, zinc, and selenium; a pre-workout with high-dose B6 and niacin; and a zinc lozenge during cold season.

No single product exceeds a UL. Together, zinc can pass 50 mg. The ODS states that 50 mg or more per day for weeks interferes with copper absorption, lowers immune function, and lowers HDL cholesterol. B6 stacks the same way. The ODS notes reports of sensory neuropathy below 500 mg per day, which is why EFSA cut its limit to 12 mg.

Vitamin A is the subtle one. The UL covers only retinol and retinyl esters. A label listing 3,000 mcg RAE with 60% from beta-carotene delivers 1,200 mcg of preformed vitamin A. Our article on whether multivitamins do anything explains why a multi is often the first product to cut.

What does real toxicity look like?

Selenium. In 2008, 201 people in 10 states developed selenosis from a liquid supplement that contained 200 times the labeled selenium (MacFarquhar et al., Arch Intern Med 2010). Diarrhea, fatigue, hair loss, and joint pain were the common symptoms. The ODS lists hair loss, brittle nails, garlic breath odor, metallic taste, and nerve symptoms as the signs of selenosis.

Vitamin D. The ODS states that vitamin D toxicity is almost always caused by supplements, not food or sun. It raises blood calcium above 11.1 mg/dL, against a normal range of 8.4 to 10.2 mg/dL. Severe cases progress to kidney failure, soft-tissue calcification, and cardiac arrhythmias. Taking calcium alongside high-dose vitamin D raises the risk. Our guide to how much vitamin D to take covers testing before dosing.

Iron. The ODS reports that at least 43 US children died between 1983 and 2000 after ingesting high-dose iron supplements. In adults, an acute intake above 20 mg/kg (about 1,365 mg for a 150-pound person) can cause corrosive damage to the intestine.

Vitamin B6 and vitamin A. B6 at 1 to 6 g per day for 12 to 40 months causes severe, progressive sensory neuropathy. Chronic excess vitamin A causes joint pain, fatigue, and abnormal liver tests, and intakes above the UL in pregnancy can cause birth defects.

How often do supplements send people to the ER?

CDC and FDA researchers analyzed surveillance data from 63 emergency departments between 2004 and 2013 (Geller et al., NEJM 2015). They estimated 23,005 emergency department visits per year (95% CI, 18,611 to 27,398) for adverse events related to dietary supplements, and 2,154 hospitalizations per year.

The pattern matters more than the total. Adults aged 20 to 34 made up 28.0% of visits, and weight-loss or energy products caused 71.8% of visits involving palpitations, chest pain, or a fast heart rate. Unsupervised children accounted for 21.2% of visits. Among adults 65 and older, choking or pill-related swallowing problems caused 37.6% of visits, and micronutrient pills were involved in 83.1% of those.

Can supplements damage your liver?

Yes, and the share of cases is rising. The US Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network (DILIN) enrolled 839 patients with liver injury between 2004 and 2013. Herbal and dietary supplements caused 130 of them, or 15.5%, and the proportion rose from 7% to 20% over the study period (Navarro et al., Hepatology 2014). Non-bodybuilding supplements led to death or liver transplant in 13% of cases, compared with 3% for prescription drugs.

Two common ingredients stand out:

  • Turmeric and curcumin. LiverTox now scores turmeric an A, a well-documented cause of liver injury, and calls it the most common cause of herbal liver injury in the United States (LiverTox: Turmeric). DILIN reported 10 cases, all since 2011, and 7 of the 10 patients carried the HLA-B*35:01 gene variant (Halegoua-DeMarzio et al., Am J Med 2023).
  • Green tea extract. Also scored A. Doses equal to 800 mg or more of EGCG per day are associated with significant liver enzyme elevations, and injury typically appears 1 to 6 months after starting (LiverTox: Green Tea).

Exposure is wide. A 2024 NHANES analysis estimated that 15.6 million US adults (4.7%) took at least one of six potentially liver-toxic botanicals in the prior 30 days, and 87.6% of turmeric users took it without a clinician's recommendation (Likhitsup et al., JAMA Netw Open 2024). Turmeric and green tea extract also appear in many greens powders and fat burners, so one person can take them from three sources at once.

Which supplement-drug interactions matter most?

Every added product is another chance to change how a prescription works. These interactions have the strongest evidence:

  • St. John's wort weakens many drugs by speeding their breakdown. The NCCIH list includes birth control pills, cyclosporine, warfarin, digoxin, simvastatin, phenytoin, indinavir, and cancer drugs such as imatinib. Combined with antidepressants, it can push serotonin to dangerous levels.
  • Vitamin K antagonizes warfarin. The ODS states that people on warfarin must keep vitamin K intake consistent, because sudden changes raise or lower the anticoagulant effect (ODS vitamin K).
  • Ginkgo biloba with warfarin is associated with more major bleeding events than warfarin alone (NCCIH herb-drug review).
  • Zinc, iron, and calcium bind quinolone and tetracycline antibiotics in the gut. Iron and calcium also block levothyroxine. Our sister site thyroid.md covers the four-hour separation rule.

How do you audit your stack?

This takes about ten minutes and catches most problems.

  1. Put every product on the table. Include greens powders, protein powders, pre-workouts, lozenges, gummies, and fortified drinks.
  2. Build a nutrient grid. List zinc, preformed vitamin A, vitamin D, B6, iron, selenium, and vitamin K down one side and products across the top. Fill in each Supplement Facts panel. Add each row.
  3. Compare each total to the UL. Any row above the UL is a problem. Any row above half the UL needs a reason.
  4. Flag the liver ingredients. Circle turmeric, curcumin, green tea extract, ashwagandha, and any fat-burner blend.
  5. Cross-check prescriptions. Bring the grid and your medication list to a pharmacist.
  6. Cut duplicates. If two products deliver the same nutrient, keep the one with a stated purpose.
  7. Check for third-party testing. The selenium outbreak happened because a product contained 200 times its label. USP or NSF certification verifies the dose. Our guide to supplement regulation explains what those marks cover.

If you develop dark urine, yellow skin or eyes, numbness in the hands or feet, hair loss, or a racing heartbeat while taking several products, stop them and see a doctor.

The bottom line

You can take too many supplements at once, and the problem is arithmetic rather than any single product. Six nutrients have published upper limits that a normal-looking stack of four products can cross. Supplements cause an estimated 23,005 emergency visits and 2,154 hospitalizations a year in the United States, and herbal products now cause about 20% of registered drug-induced liver injury cases.

Count the doses across every product. Compare each total to the UL. Give the list to a pharmacist.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you overdose on vitamins from supplements?

Yes. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements publishes Tolerable Upper Intake Levels for adults: 3,000 mcg RAE of preformed vitamin A, 100 mcg (4,000 IU) of vitamin D, 100 mg of vitamin B6, 45 mg of iron, 40 mg of zinc, and 400 mcg of selenium per day. These limits count food, fortified food, and every supplement together. A multivitamin, a greens powder, a pre-workout, and a lozenge can cross a limit even when no single product does.

How many people go to the ER because of supplements?

An estimated 23,005 emergency department visits per year in the United States are attributed to adverse events from dietary supplements, leading to about 2,154 hospitalizations per year. The estimate comes from CDC and FDA researchers who analyzed surveillance data from 63 emergency departments between 2004 and 2013 and published the results in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2015. Weight-loss and energy products caused most cardiac symptom visits among adults aged 20 to 34, and swallowing problems with large pills caused most visits among adults 65 and older.

Which supplements are most likely to damage the liver?

In the US Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network, herbal and dietary supplements caused 15.5% of liver injury cases enrolled between 2004 and 2013, and the share rose from 7% to 20% over that period. LiverTox rates turmeric and green tea extract as well-documented causes of liver injury. Turmeric is now the most common cause of herbal liver injury in the United States, and green tea extract doses equal to 800 mg or more of EGCG per day are linked to significant liver enzyme elevations. Bodybuilding products and multi-ingredient weight-loss blends are also frequently implicated.

What happens if you take too much zinc?

The adult Tolerable Upper Intake Level for zinc is 40 mg per day. According to the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, doses of 50 mg or more per day for weeks interfere with copper absorption, reduce immune function, and lower HDL cholesterol. Zinc appears in multivitamins, greens powders, immune formulas, and cold lozenges, so the total across products is what matters.

How do I check whether I am taking too many supplements?

Put every product on the table, including powders, gummies, and fortified drinks. Build a grid with zinc, preformed vitamin A, vitamin D, vitamin B6, iron, selenium, and vitamin K down one side and each product across the top. Fill in the amount from each Supplement Facts panel and add each row. Compare each total to the NIH Tolerable Upper Intake Level. Circle turmeric, green tea extract, and ashwagandha wherever they appear. Then bring the grid and your prescription list to a pharmacist to check for interactions.
