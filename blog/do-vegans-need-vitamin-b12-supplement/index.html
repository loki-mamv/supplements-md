Do Vegans Need a Vitamin B12 Supplement?

September 30, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Yes. Vegans need a reliable source of vitamin B12, and for most people that means a supplement, fortified foods, or both. B12 occurs naturally in animal foods, not in plants. The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics states plainly that vegans need fortified foods or supplements to get it.

Why can't a plant-based diet supply B12?

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is made by bacteria, not by plants or animals. Animals store it in their tissues, milk, and eggs, which is why animal foods are the natural dietary source. MedlinePlus puts it directly: vitamin B12 is not present in plant foods unless they are fortified.

This makes B12 different from almost every other nutrient a vegan diet has to plan for. Protein, iron, calcium, and zinc all come from plant foods, even if planning takes some care. B12 has no dependable plant source at all.

The 2016 Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics position paper supports well-planned vegan diets at every life stage, including pregnancy and childhood. The same paper names B12 as the exception that needs a reliable outside source. A well-planned vegan diet includes B12 by design.

How much B12 do adults need each day?

The Recommended Dietary Allowances come from the Food and Nutrition Board of the National Academies. MedlinePlus lists them as:

  • Everyone 14 and older: 2.4 micrograms (mcg) per day
  • Pregnancy: 2.6 mcg per day
  • Breastfeeding: 2.8 mcg per day
  • Children: 0.9 mcg (ages 1 to 3), 1.2 mcg (4 to 8), and 1.8 mcg (9 to 13)

These are small amounts, measured in millionths of a gram. The RDA is an intake level for healthy people who absorb B12 normally. It is not a treatment dose. People who already have a deficiency, or who have an absorption problem, need a plan from their clinician.

The body stores B12 in the liver. MedlinePlus notes that it can hold a few years' worth. That is why a new vegan can feel fine for a long time while stores slowly run down, and why "I feel fine" is not proof of adequate intake.

How common is B12 deficiency in vegans?

Common enough that it is the expected outcome without a reliable source. In the EPIC-Oxford cohort, researchers measured serum B12 in 689 British men. Mean levels were 281 pmol/L in omnivores, 182 pmol/L in vegetarians, and 122 pmol/L in vegans.

Using a cutoff of 118 pmol/L, 52% of vegans were classified as B12 deficient. Only 7% of vegetarians and one omnivore met the same cutoff. Time on the diet and age did not predict the result.

A 2013 review in Nutrition Reviews pooled 18 studies that used the more specific markers methylmalonic acid or holotranscobalamin. Reported deficiency rates were 62% in pregnant vegetarian women and 21% to 41% in adolescents. Vegans had higher rates than vegetarians. The authors concluded that vegetarians develop B12 depletion regardless of age, location, or diet type, and advised regular B12 supplements.

What happens when B12 runs low?

B12 helps make red blood cells and keeps the nervous system working. Low levels can cause a type of anemia and, over time, nerve damage. MedlinePlus lists these symptoms:

  • Fatigue, lightheadedness, and shortness of breath during exercise
  • Pale skin, loss of appetite, and a swollen, red tongue
  • Numbness and tingling in the hands and feet
  • Loss of balance, trouble concentrating, and irritability
  • Confusion or dementia, and in severe cases psychosis

The nerve symptoms are the main reason not to wait. MedlinePlus warns that long-term deficiency can cause nerve damage that may become permanent if treatment does not start promptly. Symptoms may also be mild or absent at first, so a lack of symptoms does not rule out a problem.

Diet is not the only cause. MedlinePlus also lists pernicious anemia, stomach or intestinal surgery, celiac disease, Crohn disease, long-term heartburn medicines, and long-term metformin. A vegan with one of these risks has two reasons to test. If you take prescription drugs, see which supplements interact with common medications.

Are fortified foods enough, or do you need a pill?

Either can work if intake is consistent. MedlinePlus names fortified breakfast cereals and some nutritional yeast products as sources for vegetarians. It adds that the B12 used to fortify foods is very easy to absorb.

The catch is "some." Not every cereal, plant milk, or nutritional yeast is fortified, and amounts vary by brand. Check the Nutrition Facts panel for vitamin B12 on each product. If you rely on fortified foods, you need to eat them daily, not now and then.

A supplement removes the guesswork. B12 comes in single-nutrient pills, sublingual tablets, and almost all multivitamins, per MedlinePlus. Our review of what multivitamins actually do names B12 for vegans as one of the clearest reasons to take one. Because supplements are not approved before sale, look for a product that is third-party tested so the label matches what is inside. If you track daily intake with an app, calories.md covers how nutrition tracking works.

Does spirulina, algae, or seaweed count as a B12 source?

Spirulina does not. A 1999 study of spirulina tablets found that older lab methods overstated their B12 content by about 6 to 9 times. Most of the B12-like material was pseudovitamin B12, which the human body cannot use. It made up 83% of the analogues purified.

A 2002 follow-up from the same group concluded that spirulina tablets are not suitable as a B12 source, especially for vegetarians. The same study found true B12 in chlorella and in green and purple laver (nori).

Nori looks more promising. A 2014 review in Nutrients called dried purple laver the most suitable plant-derived B12 source currently available. But content varies by product, and it is not a tested substitute for fortified foods or supplements. No major guideline we reviewed treats seaweed as a reliable B12 source.

How do you test B12 status, and which form should you choose?

The usual first test is serum B12. MedlinePlus lists serum methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine as related tests. The MedlinePlus MMA test page explains the logic: without enough B12, MMA builds up, so a high MMA level can be a sign of deficiency. It names strict vegetarians and vegans as a higher-risk group.

On labels you will see several forms. Cyanocobalamin is the form MedlinePlus lists under B12's alternative names. Methylcobalamin, adenosylcobalamin, and hydroxocobalamin are also sold. None of the sources used here tested one form against another, so treat claims that one form is "better" with caution. Timing matters less than taking it consistently; our guide to supplement timing covers the general rules.

The bottom line

Vegans need a reliable B12 source, and planning for it is part of a healthy vegan diet, not a sign the diet failed. Adults need 2.4 mcg per day, more during pregnancy and breastfeeding. Fortified foods or a supplement can meet that. Spirulina cannot. Because liver stores hide a shortfall for years and nerve damage can become permanent, ask your clinician about a serum B12 or MMA test if you have been vegan for a while without a steady source.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get enough B12 from nutritional yeast?

Only if the product is fortified with B12. MedlinePlus says some nutritional yeast products contain added B12, but not all do. Check the Nutrition Facts panel on the exact product you buy.

Do vegetarians who eat eggs and dairy also need B12?

They get some B12 from eggs and dairy, but many still run low. In the EPIC-Oxford cohort, 7% of vegetarian men were classified as deficient, compared with 52% of vegans. A fortified food or supplement is a reasonable backstop, and testing can settle the question.

How long does it take to become B12 deficient after going vegan?

It varies. The liver can store a few years' worth of B12, so a shortfall may not show up for a long time. In EPIC-Oxford, time on a vegan diet did not predict serum B12 levels, so do not assume you are safe because you switched recently.

Is too much B12 from a supplement dangerous?

B12 is water-soluble, and MedlinePlus notes that leftover amounts of water-soluble vitamins leave the body in the urine. Talk to your clinician before taking high doses, especially if you have a medical condition or take prescription drugs.

Do vegan children and pregnant women need special attention?

Yes. B12 needs rise to 2.6 mcg a day in pregnancy and 2.8 mcg while breastfeeding. A review of 18 studies reported deficiency in 62% of pregnant vegetarian women and in 25% to almost 86% of vegetarian children, so these groups should plan B12 with a clinician.
