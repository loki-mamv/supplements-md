Omega-3 Fatty Acids: What the Science Actually Says

February 2026

Fish oil capsules are the second most popular supplement in the United States after multivitamins, taken by roughly 19 million adults according to the National Center for Health Statistics. The supplement industry markets omega-3s for heart health, brain function, joint pain, inflammation, skin, mood, and just about everything else. The clinical evidence paints a more selective picture -- one where the benefits are real but narrower than advertising suggests.

The Basics: EPA, DHA, and ALA

Omega-3 fatty acids are polyunsaturated fats that the body cannot synthesize on its own. The three principal forms are:

  • EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid): Found in fatty fish and algae. Primarily involved in reducing inflammation and triglyceride synthesis.
  • DHA (docosahexaenoic acid): Found in fatty fish and algae. A structural component of brain tissue, retinal cells, and cell membranes.
  • ALA (alpha-linolenic acid): Found in flaxseed, chia seeds, walnuts, and canola oil. A precursor that the body can convert to EPA and DHA, but the conversion rate is poor -- typically less than 5% for EPA and less than 0.5% for DHA.

This conversion inefficiency is why most clinical research focuses on EPA and DHA from marine sources, and why plant-based ALA supplements are generally not considered equivalent to fish oil.

Cardiovascular Benefits: The Strongest Evidence

Triglyceride Reduction

This is the clearest, least contested benefit. Omega-3 fatty acids at doses of 2-4 grams per day reduce triglyceride levels by 15-30%. This effect is strong enough that the FDA has approved prescription omega-3 products (Vascepa, Lovaza) specifically for hypertriglyceridemia. Over-the-counter fish oil at standard doses (1 gram total omega-3) produces more modest triglyceride reduction of about 5-10%.

Cardiovascular Events

This is where it gets complicated. The story has shifted over the past two decades:

Early studies like GISSI-Prevenzione (1999) showed significant cardiovascular benefit in patients with recent heart attacks. The JELIS trial (2007) in Japan found a 19% reduction in major coronary events with EPA added to statin therapy.

But then came a string of null results. The ASCEND trial (2018) in diabetic patients and the VITAL trial (2019) in the general population both found no significant cardiovascular benefit from standard-dose omega-3 supplementation (1 gram/day).

Then REDUCE-IT (2019) changed the conversation. This trial of 8,179 patients with elevated triglycerides found that high-dose purified EPA (icosapent ethyl, 4 grams/day) reduced major cardiovascular events by 25%. Critics noted the use of mineral oil placebo, which may have inflated the treatment effect by raising LDL in the control group. The STRENGTH trial, using a different placebo (corn oil), found no benefit for a combined EPA/DHA product.

The emerging consensus: standard-dose fish oil (1-2 grams of combined EPA and DHA) likely does not meaningfully reduce cardiovascular events in the general population. High-dose purified EPA (4 grams) may benefit people with elevated triglycerides already on statin therapy, but this is a prescription product, not an over-the-counter supplement.

Brain Health and Cognitive Function

DHA constitutes about 40% of the polyunsaturated fatty acids in the brain, which has made it an obvious candidate for cognitive health research. The results have been largely underwhelming for supplementation in healthy adults.

A 2012 Cochrane review of three randomized trials (3,536 healthy older adults) found no benefit of omega-3 supplementation on cognitive function over 6-40 months. The authors' conclusion was unambiguous: "Direct evidence on the effect of omega-3 PUFA on incident dementia is lacking."

There is somewhat more interesting data for people with mild cognitive impairment (MCI). A 2020 meta-analysis in the Journal of Alzheimer's Disease found modest improvements in specific cognitive domains with DHA supplementation in MCI patients, but not in those with established dementia. The effect sizes were small.

For prenatal brain development, the evidence is stronger. Adequate DHA intake during pregnancy is associated with better neurodevelopmental outcomes. Most prenatal vitamins now include DHA, and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommends at least 200 mg of DHA daily during pregnancy.

Depression and Mood

A 2019 meta-analysis published in Translational Psychiatry (26 RCTs, 2,160 participants) found a small but statistically significant benefit of omega-3 supplementation for major depressive disorder, with EPA-predominant formulations performing better than DHA-predominant ones. The effect size was modest -- comparable to a mild antidepressant effect -- and the study quality was mixed.

The American Psychiatric Association considers omega-3s a reasonable adjunctive treatment for mood disorders but does not recommend them as standalone therapy. If you have clinical depression, omega-3s are not a substitute for established treatments.

Joint Health and Inflammation

For rheumatoid arthritis, several systematic reviews have found that fish oil supplementation (at least 2.7 grams of EPA+DHA daily) modestly reduces joint tenderness and morning stiffness, and may allow some patients to reduce NSAID use. The benefit for osteoarthritis is less clear, with most trials showing minimal or no effect.

Quality and Contamination Concerns

Fish oil supplements can degrade through oxidation, producing fishy taste and potentially harmful lipid peroxides. A 2015 study in the Journal of Nutritional Science tested 32 commercial fish oil products and found that the majority exceeded recommended oxidation levels. Look for products with third-party testing (IFOS, USP, NSF) that verify both potency and oxidation markers.

Mercury contamination is less of a concern than commonly believed. The molecular distillation process used in reputable fish oil manufacturing effectively removes mercury and other heavy metals. Testing by ConsumerLab and IFOS has consistently found mercury levels well below safety thresholds in most major brands.

Drug Interactions

High-dose omega-3s (above 3 grams/day) may increase bleeding time and should be used cautiously with anticoagulants (warfarin, apixaban) and antiplatelet drugs (aspirin, clopidogrel). Inform your prescriber if you take fish oil alongside blood-thinning medications. Standard doses of 1-2 grams are generally not a concern.

Practical Recommendations

  1. Eat fish first. Two servings of fatty fish per week (salmon, mackerel, sardines, herring) provide adequate EPA and DHA for most people and deliver protein, selenium, and vitamin D alongside the omega-3s.
  2. If you supplement, choose quality. Look for IFOS, USP, or NSF certification. Store in the refrigerator to slow oxidation. If it smells strongly of fish, it may be rancid.
  3. Standard dose: 500-1,000 mg of combined EPA+DHA daily. This approximates the intake from two fish meals per week.
  4. For specific conditions (high triglycerides, rheumatoid arthritis), higher doses (2-4 grams) may be warranted but should be discussed with a physician.
  5. Vegetarians and vegans can use algae-derived EPA+DHA supplements, which provide the same omega-3s without the fish.

The Bottom Line

Omega-3 supplementation is best supported for triglyceride reduction and as an adjunct in inflammatory conditions like rheumatoid arthritis. For general heart health and brain function in healthy people, the evidence for over-the-counter fish oil is weak. Eating fish is more consistently beneficial than taking fish oil capsules. If you do not eat fish, an algae-based supplement providing 500 mg of EPA+DHA is a reasonable option.
