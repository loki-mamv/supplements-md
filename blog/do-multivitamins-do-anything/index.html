Do Multivitamins Actually Do Anything?

August 23, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: for a healthy, well-fed adult, a daily multivitamin does not appear to extend life. A 2024 analysis of 390,124 US adults followed for more than 20 years found no reduction in death from any cause, from cancer, or from heart disease among regular multivitamin users. The US Preventive Services Task Force reached the same conclusion in 2022 and rated the evidence insufficient. Multivitamins still do specific, real jobs for specific people: preventing neural tube defects, correcting documented deficiencies, and covering restricted diets. "Insurance against a bad diet" is not one of those jobs.

About one in three US adults takes one. The gap between that number and the evidence is what this article is about.

What does the largest study show?

In June 2024, researchers at the National Cancer Institute published an analysis in JAMA Network Open pooling three large prospective cohorts: NIH-AARP, PLCO, and the Agricultural Health Study. It covered 390,124 generally healthy US adults with more than two decades of follow-up (Loftfield et al., JAMA Netw Open 2024).

Daily multivitamin users were not less likely to die than non-users. That held for all-cause mortality and for death from cancer, heart disease, and cerebrovascular disease separately. The authors specifically excluded people with a history of cancer or major chronic disease at baseline, and they accounted for the tendency of sicker people to start taking supplements, which had muddied earlier observational work.

The NIH summary of the study put it plainly: no mortality benefit in healthy adults.

What do the guidelines say?

The USPSTF 2022 statement on vitamin and mineral supplementation for preventing cardiovascular disease and cancer split its findings three ways:

  • Multivitamins: Grade I. Evidence insufficient to weigh benefits against harms.
  • Single or paired nutrients (other than the two below): Grade I. Same verdict.
  • Beta-carotene and vitamin E: Grade D. Recommended against. For beta-carotene the Task Force concluded with moderate certainty that harms outweigh benefits. For vitamin E it concluded with moderate certainty that there is no net benefit.

That Grade D matters. It is not "we do not know." It is a recommendation to stop, and it applies to two nutrients that appear in plenty of formulations marketed for heart and cancer protection.

Is there any positive evidence?

Yes, in one domain, and it deserves an honest hearing rather than dismissal.

The COSMOS trial randomized older adults to a daily multivitamin (Centrum Silver) or placebo. In the COSMOS-Web arm, 3,562 participants took annual internet-based neuropsychological tests over three years. The multivitamin group performed better on memory recall at the one-year primary endpoint, and the difference persisted through year three. Pooling three cognitive substudies within COSMOS, the investigators estimated that daily multivitamin use slowed cognitive aging by roughly 1.8 years over the three-year period.

Two caveats belong next to that number. The effect sizes are small and were measured on test scores, not on diagnoses of dementia or on anything a person would notice day to day. And the same trial program found no mortality or cancer benefit. A modest, replicated signal on memory tests in adults over 60 is what the evidence supports. Anything broader is a stretch.

Who actually benefits from a multivitamin?

The case for supplementation is strongest when it targets a specific nutrient in a specific person, not when it sprays 26 ingredients at a healthy adult.

Anyone who could become pregnant

This is the clearest indication in the entire category. The CDC advises 400 micrograms of folic acid daily for all women and teens who could become pregnant, because the neural tube closes in the first weeks after conception, often before a pregnancy is recognized. This is prevention with a defined mechanism and a defined window.

People following restricted diets

Vegans need a reliable vitamin B12 source, because B12 occurs naturally only in animal foods. Absence of that intake produces a deficiency that takes years to appear and can cause irreversible neurological damage if it is missed.

Older adults and people on long-term metformin

Stomach acid production falls with age, which reduces absorption of food-bound B12. Long-term metformin use is also associated with lower B12 levels. Both groups warrant testing rather than assumption.

People after bariatric surgery, or with malabsorption

Celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, chronic pancreatitis, and bariatric procedures all impair absorption. Supplementation here is treatment, and it is usually specific, high-dose, and monitored.

Documented deficiencies

Low iron, low vitamin D, and low B12 are diagnosed with a blood test and corrected with a targeted dose. A multivitamin typically contains too little to correct a real deficiency, which is one reason it makes a poor substitute for testing. Our guides to vitamin D testing and how much vitamin D to take cover that distinction in detail.

Can a multivitamin hurt you?

Rarely, but not never, and the failure modes are worth knowing.

The fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K accumulate. Stacking a multivitamin on top of a separate vitamin D capsule and a fortified drink is how people quietly reach intakes they never intended. High-dose folic acid can mask the blood signs of a vitamin B12 deficiency while the neurological damage continues underneath, which is why B12 belongs alongside folate rather than folate alone.

Beta-carotene carries the strongest warning in the category. Large randomized trials in smokers found an increase in lung cancer, and that finding drives the Grade D recommendation above.

There are also interactions. Vitamin K affects warfarin dosing, calcium and iron interfere with thyroid medication and with several antibiotics, and high-dose vitamin E has an antiplatelet effect. Our supplement interactions guide covers the common pairs.

One structural point is easy to miss: in the United States, supplements are regulated as food, not as drugs. Manufacturers do not need to prove efficacy or purity before selling a product. Our guide to supplement regulation explains what third-party testing marks like USP and NSF actually verify.

What should you do?

A reasonable sequence for most people:

  1. Name the reason. If you cannot state which nutrient you are targeting and why, that is the finding, not the starting point.
  2. Test before treating if you suspect a deficiency. Ferritin, vitamin D, and B12 are inexpensive and change what you should take.
  3. Take folic acid if you could become pregnant, regardless of how good your diet is.
  4. Check your formulation for beta-carotene and high-dose vitamin E, particularly if you smoke or used to.
  5. Bring the bottle to appointments so interactions get caught.

The bottom line

A daily multivitamin in a healthy, well-fed adult is not a life-extension tool. The largest analysis to date, covering 390,124 people over two decades, found no mortality benefit, and the USPSTF rated the prevention evidence insufficient in 2022.

What survives the evidence is narrower and more useful: folic acid before and during early pregnancy, B12 for vegans and some older adults, targeted correction of tested deficiencies, and a modest memory signal in adults over 60 from one randomized trial program. That is a real list. It is just much shorter than the label implies.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do multivitamins help you live longer?

No, not in healthy adults. A 2024 JAMA Network Open analysis pooled three US cohorts covering 390,124 generally healthy adults with more than 20 years of follow-up and found no reduction in all-cause mortality, cancer mortality, or cardiovascular mortality among daily multivitamin users.

What does the USPSTF say about multivitamins?

In its 2022 statement, the US Preventive Services Task Force gave multivitamins a Grade I for preventing cardiovascular disease and cancer, meaning the evidence is insufficient to weigh benefits against harms. It gave beta-carotene and vitamin E a Grade D, recommending against their use, because harms outweigh benefits for beta-carotene and there is no net benefit for vitamin E.

Do multivitamins improve memory?

One randomized trial program found a small effect. In the COSMOS-Web study, 3,562 older adults took a daily multivitamin or placebo and completed annual cognitive tests for three years. The multivitamin group scored better on memory recall at one year, and the effect persisted. Pooling three cognitive substudies, investigators estimated the equivalent of about 1.8 years of slowed cognitive aging over three years. The effects were measured on test scores, not on dementia diagnoses.

Who should take a multivitamin?

The clearest cases are people who could become pregnant, who need 400 micrograms of folic acid daily to reduce neural tube defects; vegans, who need a reliable vitamin B12 source; older adults and long-term metformin users, who absorb B12 less well; people after bariatric surgery or with malabsorption conditions; and anyone with a deficiency confirmed by a blood test.

Can multivitamins be harmful?

Occasionally. Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K accumulate, so stacking products can push intake higher than intended. High-dose folic acid can mask the blood signs of a vitamin B12 deficiency while nerve damage continues. Beta-carotene increased lung cancer risk in smokers in large randomized trials. Vitamin K, calcium, iron, and high-dose vitamin E also interact with common prescription drugs.
