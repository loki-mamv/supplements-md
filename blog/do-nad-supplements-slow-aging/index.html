Do NAD+ Supplements Slow Aging?

October 10, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida
Table of Contents

  6. Are NAD+ supplements safe?
  7. Is NMN legal as a dietary supplement?

The short answer: No human trial shows that NAD+ supplements slow aging. Nicotinamide riboside (NR) and nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) do raise NAD+ in blood. A 2024 meta-analysis of 12 randomized trials found that most clinically relevant outcomes did not differ from control. The lifespan result comes from mice, and a later mouse study did not confirm it.

What is NAD+ and why do people take it?

NAD+ stands for nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide. Cells use it in energy metabolism. Researchers describe it as a co-substrate for enzymes linked to the benefits of calorie restriction in animals.

The aging claim rests on one idea: NAD+ falls with age, so topping it up should help. The human evidence for that first step is thinner than the marketing suggests. A 2019 trial report noted that NAD+ decline with aging was reported "in preclinical models, but human data are sparse."

The trials below did not test NAD+ itself. They tested precursors, which are building blocks the body converts to NAD+. NR and NMN are the two best studied. NR is a member of the vitamin B3 family, the same family as niacin.

What did the animal studies show?

Animal studies started the interest, and they do not agree with each other.

  • The positive study. A 2016 paper in Science reported that NR rejuvenated muscle stem cells in aged mice. The authors also reported that NR "increases mouse life span."
  • The later study. A 2021 report in Aging Cell tested NR and several other compounds in genetically mixed male and female mice. NR did not increase lifespan significantly in either sex at the dose tested.

Mouse results often fail to carry over to people. Here the mouse lifespan result did not even hold up in a second mouse study. No human trial has measured lifespan.

Do NR and NMN raise NAD+ in humans?

Yes. This part of the claim holds up.

  • NR in healthy adults. A 2018 crossover trial gave NR or placebo for 6 weeks each to healthy middle-aged and older adults. NR was well tolerated and "effectively stimulates NAD+ metabolism."
  • NR in muscle. A 2019 trial in 12 aged men used 1 g of NR per day for 21 days. NR raised NAD+ related compounds in muscle tissue.
  • NMN in blood. A 2022 trial of 80 middle-aged adults used placebo or 300 mg, 600 mg, or 900 mg of NMN daily for 60 days. Blood NAD rose in every NMN group compared with placebo.

A higher blood NAD+ level is a lab result. It is not proof of slower aging. The question that matters is whether health outcomes change.

Do they change health outcomes in people?

Mostly no, with a few small positive signals.

Trials that found no benefit. Danish researchers gave 40 obese, insulin-resistant men either placebo or 1,000 mg of NR twice daily for 12 weeks. Insulin sensitivity did not improve. NR also had no effect on resting energy expenditure or body composition.

The 2019 muscle trial found the same pattern. NR reached the muscle, and mitochondrial energy production did not change. That trial did report lower levels of circulating inflammatory markers.

Trials that found a benefit. A 10-week trial in Science studied postmenopausal women with prediabetes who were overweight or obese. Muscle insulin sensitivity increased after NMN and did not change after placebo.

In the 80-person NMN trial, the NMN groups walked farther in a six-minute walking test than the placebo group. The insulin resistance score did not differ from placebo. The trial lasted 60 days, and its first author listed a company affiliation. Results like these need independent confirmation.

None of these trials measured the outcomes people mean by "aging": disease, disability, or death. They ran for weeks, not years.

What do systematic reviews conclude?

Reviews that pool the randomized trials are cautious.

  • Metabolic health. A 2024 meta-analysis covered 12 trials and 513 participants. NMN raised blood NAD levels. Most clinically relevant outcomes were not significantly different from control. Every trial had some concerns or a high risk of bias. The authors wrote that "an exaggeration of the benefits of NMN supplementation may exist in the field."
  • Blood sugar and cholesterol. A second 2024 meta-analysis pooled 8 trials with 342 adults. It found no significant benefit of NMN on fasting glucose, insulin, HbA1c, or lipid profile.
  • Muscle. A 2025 meta-analysis looked at adults with a mean age above 60. It concluded that current evidence "does not support NMN and NR supplementation for preserving muscle mass and function."
  • Newest data. A 2026 meta-analysis of 15 trials found no significant effect on body weight, fasting glucose, HbA1c, lipids, or systolic blood pressure. Diastolic blood pressure fell slightly.

Compare this with supplements that have stronger trial support. Our list of supplements that actually work covers those. For habits that affect healthy aging, such as sleep, movement, and diet, see thrive.md.

Are NAD+ supplements safe?

Short-term safety looks acceptable. Long-term safety is unknown.

The 2026 meta-analysis included trials that used 250 to 2,000 mg of NMN per day for 14 days to 24 weeks. NMN did not increase adverse events or serious adverse events. It did not raise the liver enzymes ALT or AST. The authors asked for larger and longer trials to confirm long-term safety.

The NR trials report similar results over short periods. The 12-week NR trial saw no serious adverse events due to NR, and safety blood tests were normal.

Niacin is different. Niacin (nicotinic acid) is another B3 form. MedlinePlus says niacin causes flushing: redness, warmth, itching, and tingling of the face, neck, chest, or back. MedlinePlus also lists liver damage, increased blood sugar, peptic ulcers, and skin rashes as effects of too much niacin. It says the nicotinamide form does not appear to cause these side effects. Check the label to see which form a product contains.

Labels are not always accurate. Our guide to what third-party tested means explains how to check a product.

Is NMN legal as a dietary supplement?

Yes, as of October 2026. The status changed twice.

  • October 2022. The FDA told a manufacturer that NMN was excluded from the definition of a dietary supplement. The reason was that NMN had been authorized for investigation as a new drug.
  • September 2025. The FDA reversed that position in its response to a citizen petition, posted to the federal docket on September 29, 2025. The agency wrote, "we now conclude that NMN is not excluded" from the dietary supplement definition. It cited evidence that NMN was sold as a supplement in the United States before the drug investigation was authorized.

The letter is about legal status. It does not say that NMN works. The same letter notes that when the FDA files a new ingredient notification, that step "does not constitute a finding by FDA" that the ingredient is safe. Our explainer on how supplements are regulated covers the rules.

The bottom line

NR and NMN raise NAD+ in blood. That is the only claim the human trials support well. Trials lasting weeks to months show few changes in health outcomes, and pooled analyses find no clear benefit for blood sugar, cholesterol, or muscle. No trial has tested whether these products extend life or delay age-related disease in people.

Short-term side effects appear uncommon. Nobody has safety data covering years of use.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is NMN better than NR?

No trial cited here compared them head to head for a health outcome. Both raise NAD+ in blood. A 2025 meta-analysis found that neither one preserved muscle mass or function in adults with a mean age above 60.

How long does it take for NMN to raise NAD+ levels?

In a 2022 trial of 80 middle-aged adults, blood NAD was higher than placebo at day 30 and at day 60. The trial used 300 mg, 600 mg, or 900 mg of NMN daily. A higher blood level did not come with a better insulin resistance score.

Did the FDA ban NMN?

Not now. In October 2022 the FDA said NMN was excluded from the definition of a dietary supplement. In a response posted on September 29, 2025, the agency reversed that view and concluded that NMN is not excluded.

Do NAD+ supplements cause flushing like niacin?

Flushing is a known effect of niacin, also called nicotinic acid. MedlinePlus describes it as redness, warmth, itching, and tingling. MedlinePlus says the nicotinamide form does not appear to cause the side effects of niacin. Trials of NR and NMN reported good short-term tolerability.

Can NMN or NR help you live longer?

Nobody knows, because no human trial has measured lifespan. One 2016 mouse study reported longer life with NR. A 2021 mouse study found no significant lifespan increase with NR in either sex.
