Should You Take Prenatal Vitamins If You Are Not Pregnant?

October 12, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: It depends on whether you could become pregnant. If you could, a prenatal is a reasonable way to get the 400 mcg of folic acid that the CDC and the USPSTF recommend every day. If you cannot become pregnant, a prenatal offers no proven extra benefit, and its 27 mg of iron is more than three times what men and postmenopausal women need.

What makes a prenatal different from a regular multivitamin?

A prenatal is a multivitamin built around the needs of pregnancy. Two nutrients set it apart: iron and folic acid.

  • Iron. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) says you need 18 mg of iron a day when you are not pregnant and 27 mg a day when you are. It adds that most prenatal vitamins supply the 27 mg. The FDA Daily Value used on standard supplement labels is 18 mg.
  • Folic acid. ACOG advises a prenatal with at least 400 mcg. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements (ODS) notes that prenatal products in the United States typically hold 800 or 1,000 mcg. It calls that much higher than the 400 mcg recommended to lower the risk of neural tube defects.
  • Vitamin A. The same ODS fact sheet says prenatal supplements generally provide no vitamin A in the form of retinol. Excess preformed vitamin A in pregnancy can raise the risk of birth defects.

Prenatals also have gaps. ACOG notes that choline is not found in most prenatal vitamins, and ODS reports that many prenatal multivitamins contain little or no iodine or choline. For what an ordinary multivitamin does, see do multivitamins do anything.

Who benefits from a prenatal when not pregnant?

Anyone who could become pregnant, planned or not. This is the one group with a clear, tested reason to take one.

The CDC says that if you could become pregnant, you should get 400 mcg of folic acid daily. It explains that neural tube defects happen in the first few weeks of pregnancy, often before you know you are pregnant. The CDC adds that the advice holds even if you are not planning a pregnancy.

The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) gives its highest grade, an A, to a daily supplement with 0.4 to 0.8 mg (400 to 800 mcg) of folic acid. The recommendation covers everyone who plans to or could become pregnant. Supplementation should start at least 1 month before conception and continue through the first 2 to 3 months of pregnancy. The task force notes that nearly half of all pregnancies in the United States are unplanned.

A prenatal is one way to meet that target. It is not the only way. The USPSTF wording is "a daily supplement or multivitamin" with that amount of folic acid, so a standard multivitamin or a plain folic acid tablet also counts. The recommendation does not cover people who had a previous pregnancy affected by a neural tube defect. They need individual advice from their doctor.

Do prenatal vitamins make hair and nails grow?

There is no good evidence that they do in people who are not deficient.

The closest evidence is on biotin, the vitamin most often credited for the effect. A systematic review in Skin Appendage Disorders found 18 reported cases of biotin use for hair and nail changes. In every case, the patient had an underlying condition that caused the poor growth. The authors concluded there is a lack of sufficient evidence for supplementation in healthy individuals.

A nutrient deficiency is different. ODS notes that premenopausal women are at risk of getting too little iron, and a real deficiency needs treatment. That calls for a blood test, not a guess. Hair loss also has other possible causes, including thyroid disease, which our sister site thyroid.md covers.

Can the extra iron cause problems?

Yes, for some people. Iron is the main reason a prenatal is a poor fit for those who do not need it.

MedlinePlus lists the recommended iron intake as 8 mg a day for men aged 19 and older and for women aged 51 and older. Women aged 19 to 50 need 18 mg. A prenatal with 27 mg gives a man or a postmenopausal woman more than three times the recommended amount before food is counted.

The ODS iron fact sheet sets the Tolerable Upper Intake Level for adults at 45 mg a day from all sources. One prenatal stays under that limit. A prenatal plus a separate iron pill or heavily fortified foods may not. ODS also reports these points:

  • High-dose iron supplements can cause gastric upset, constipation, nausea, abdominal pain, vomiting, and diarrhea.
  • Supplements with 25 mg of iron or more can reduce zinc absorption.
  • People with hereditary hemochromatosis absorb too much iron and are at risk of iron overload. Liver specialists advise them to avoid iron supplements.
  • Iron supplements may interact with medications, including levodopa and levothyroxine.

There is also a household risk. The MedlinePlus iron drug page warns that accidental overdose of products containing iron is a leading cause of fatal poisoning in children under 6. Keep any prenatal out of their reach. If you do need iron, our guide to taking iron every other day versus daily covers how to take it.

Can you get too much folic acid?

You can, mostly by stacking products. The ODS folate fact sheet sets the adult upper limit for folic acid from supplements and fortified foods at 1,000 mcg a day. Folate that occurs naturally in food does not count toward it.

The concern is vitamin B12. ODS explains that large amounts of folate can correct the anemia caused by B12 deficiency but not the nerve damage. A deficiency could then go unnoticed while the damage continues. MedlinePlus calls the risk low, but advises against more than 1,000 mcg a day unless a doctor directs it.

A prenatal with 800 or 1,000 mcg sits at or near that limit on its own. Add fortified foods or a second supplement that contains folic acid, and the total can pass it. Our article on whether you can take too many supplements explains how upper limits add up across products.

Who should choose something other than a prenatal?

These groups have less to gain and more reason for caution:

  • Men. The folic acid recommendation does not apply, and the iron is well above the 8 mg they need.
  • Postmenopausal women. Iron needs drop to 8 mg a day after age 50. ODS notes that multivitamins made for older adults often contain little or no iron.
  • People with hemochromatosis or high iron stores. Extra iron adds to the overload.
  • People taking levothyroxine or levodopa. Ask a pharmacist how to space iron-containing products.
  • Anyone taking a prenatal only for hair or nails. The evidence does not support it.

ACOG also warns against taking more than one serving of a prenatal a day. Doubling up does not fix a deficiency faster and raises the dose of every ingredient.

The bottom line

If you could become pregnant, a daily supplement with 400 to 800 mcg of folic acid is recommended, and a prenatal is one acceptable way to get it. If you cannot become pregnant, a prenatal has no proven advantage over a standard multivitamin, and its iron is more than you need. Check the label for iron and folic acid amounts, count your other supplements, and ask your doctor or pharmacist if you take prescription medication.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can men take prenatal vitamins?

There is no proven benefit for men. Adult men need 8 mg of iron a day, and most prenatals supply 27 mg. A multivitamin with little or no iron is a better match.

How long before trying to conceive should you start folic acid?

The USPSTF says to start at least 1 month before conception and to continue through the first 2 to 3 months of pregnancy. The CDC advises 400 mcg every day for anyone who could become pregnant, because neural tube defects form in the first few weeks, often before a pregnancy is known.

Is a regular multivitamin enough if you might become pregnant?

It can be, if the label shows 400 to 800 mcg of folic acid. The USPSTF recommendation refers to a daily supplement or multivitamin with that amount, not to a prenatal specifically. Check the Supplement Facts panel before you rely on it.

Why do prenatal vitamins cause constipation or nausea?

Iron is the usual cause. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements lists constipation, nausea, gastric upset, and abdominal pain among the effects of high-dose iron supplements. It notes that taking iron with food can help reduce them.

Can you take a prenatal and a multivitamin together?

It is not a good idea without advice from a clinician. Both contain iron and folic acid, and the adult upper limits are 45 mg of iron and 1,000 mcg of folic acid a day. ACOG also advises no more than one serving of a prenatal each day.
