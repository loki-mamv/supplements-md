What Does Third-Party Tested Mean on a Supplement Label?

The short answer: "Third-party tested" means a lab outside the company checked the product. The phrase alone does not say who tested it, what they tested for, or whether it passed. A named seal from a program like USP, NSF, Informed Sport, or ConsumerLab means the product met that program's published criteria. Those criteria cover label accuracy and contaminants. They do not cover whether the supplement works.

What does "third-party tested" actually mean?

A first party is the brand. A second party is someone with a stake in the sale, like a supplier. A third party is an outside group with no ownership in the product. When a label says "third-party tested," it claims that an outside lab analyzed the product.

That claim can mean very different things. It can mean a full certification program with facility audits and repeat testing. It can also mean a single test of one ingredient, paid for by the brand, with results nobody else can see. The words on their own do not tell you which one you are looking at.

The useful version is a named certification seal. A seal ties the product to a specific program with written standards and a public list of certified products. That list lets you confirm the claim yourself.

Doesn't the FDA already check supplements?

Not before they go on sale. The FDA states that under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA), it "does not have the authority to approve dietary supplements before they are marketed" (FDA). A company generally does not have to show the FDA its safety evidence before or after it sells a product. The main exception is a product with a new dietary ingredient, which needs a notice to the FDA at least 75 days before sale.

The FDA does set manufacturing rules. The supplement current good manufacturing practice (cGMP) rule, 21 CFR Part 111, requires each maker to set specifications for the "identity, purity, strength, and composition" of every finished batch, plus limits on contamination (21 CFR 111.70). But the company writes those specifications and runs its own checks. The FDA enforces the rule mostly through inspections and action after problems appear.

Problems do appear. An analysis of the FDA's tainted supplement database found 776 adulterated products from 146 companies between 2007 and 2016 (Tucker et al., JAMA Network Open, 2018). Most were sold for sexual enhancement (45.5%), weight loss (40.9%), or muscle building (11.9%). The hidden ingredients included sildenafil, sibutramine, and synthetic steroids. The FDA warns that its own list "covers only a small fraction" of contaminated products, and that such products turn up on Amazon, eBay, and Walmart (FDA). Independent testing tries to fill that gap. What do the major seals check for?

Each program sets its own criteria. Here is what each one says about itself on its own site.

  • USP Verified. The U.S. Pharmacopeia says its mark means the product contains the listed ingredients "in the declared potency and amounts," does not contain harmful levels of specified contaminants, breaks down and releases within a set time, and was made under FDA cGMPs (USP). USP names heavy metals such as lead and mercury, microbes, and pesticides as examples of contaminants.
  • NSF/ANSI 173 and NSF Certified for Sport. NSF describes NSF/ANSI 173 as its independent testing standard for dietary supplements. Its Certified for Sport program adds a screen for banned substances. NSF says certified products do not contain any of 290 substances banned by major athletic organizations, match their labels, have no unsafe levels of contaminants, and come from a GMP-certified facility that NSF audits (NSF Certified for Sport). NSF states the program is recognized by the U.S. Anti-Doping Agency, Major League Baseball, the NHL, and the Canadian Football League.
  • Informed Sport. This program, run by LGC Group, focuses on banned substances in sports supplements. It says it tests every batch of a certified product for banned substances before release (Informed Sport). It also warns that a label saying "Approved by WADA" is meaningless, because anti-doping bodies do not approve supplements.
  • ConsumerLab. ConsumerLab tests for identity, strength (the amount claimed), purity (specified contaminants), and disintegration (ConsumerLab). For its product reviews, it buys products at retail and does not accept samples from makers. Brands that use its seal must pass retesting every 12 months on a sample bought on the open market. ConsumerLab says its revenue comes mainly from memberships, and also from certification fees and seal licensing.

Note the difference in focus. USP and NSF/ANSI 173 center on label accuracy, contaminants, and manufacturing. NSF Certified for Sport and Informed Sport add a banned-substance screen, which matters mostly for tested athletes.

What does a seal not tell you?

A seal is a quality check, not a health endorsement. None of the programs above claim that a certified product works for its advertised purpose. A USP Verified vitamin contains what the label says. That says nothing about whether you need the vitamin.

A seal also does not make a product safe for you personally. A pure, accurately labeled supplement can still interact with a prescription drug or be too much at high doses. See our pages on supplements that interact with prescription drugs and taking too many supplements.

Finally, certification applies to specific products, not whole brands. A company can have one certified product and many that are not. Programs also differ in how often they retest, so a seal reflects the program's schedule, not a test of the exact bottle in your hand.

How can you check a testing claim yourself?

  1. Look for a named seal. "Lab tested" or "third-party tested" with no program name is hard to verify. A USP, NSF, Informed Sport, or ConsumerLab mark gives you something to check.
  2. Search the program's product list. Each of these programs lists its certified products on its website. Match the exact product name, form, and size. Informed Sport also lets you search by batch number.
  3. Ask the brand for a certificate of analysis. A certificate of analysis (COA) is a lab report for a batch. Check that it names the lab, matches your lot number, and lists results against limits.
  4. Be wary of high-risk categories. The FDA data show that sexual enhancement, weight loss, and muscle building products carry the most hidden-drug findings. A seal matters most there.
  5. Talk to a clinician or pharmacist. The FDA advises talking to a health care professional before you buy a product for a health concern (FDA).

The bottom line

"Third-party tested" is only as good as the name behind it. The FDA does not approve supplements before sale, and hidden drugs still show up in some products. A named seal from USP, NSF, Informed Sport, or ConsumerLab means an outside group checked label accuracy and contaminants under its own published rules. Confirm the product on the program's list, and remember that a seal says nothing about whether the supplement works or suits you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is "third-party tested" the same as FDA approved?

No. The FDA does not approve dietary supplements before they are sold. Third-party testing is a voluntary check that a company arranges with an outside lab or certification program.

Which supplement seal is best?

It depends on what you need. USP Verified and NSF/ANSI 173 focus on label accuracy, contaminants, and manufacturing. Athletes who face drug testing should look for NSF Certified for Sport or Informed Sport, which also screen for banned substances.

Does a third-party seal mean a supplement works?

No. The major programs check whether a product contains what its label says and is free of specified contaminants. None of them claim to test whether the supplement delivers its advertised health benefit.

How do I know a seal on a label is real?

Search the certifying program's website for the exact product name and form. USP, NSF, Informed Sport, and ConsumerLab all publish lists of certified products. If the product is not listed, treat the seal with caution.

What is a certificate of analysis for a supplement?

A certificate of analysis is a lab report for a specific production batch. A useful one names the lab, matches the lot number on your bottle, and shows test results against set limits.
