Do You Need Electrolyte Supplements If You Are Not an Athlete?

October 3, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida
Table of Contents

The short answer: Most healthy adults who are not athletes do not need electrolyte supplements. Food and drink supply electrolytes, and the average American already eats more sodium than the federal limit. Electrolyte products can help in specific cases: prolonged heavy sweating, vomiting or diarrhea, and some medical conditions under a clinician's care. People with high blood pressure, kidney disease, or heart failure should ask a clinician first.

What are electrolytes, and where do you get them?

MedlinePlus defines electrolytes as minerals that carry an electric charge when dissolved in water or body fluids, including blood. The main ones are sodium, potassium, chloride, calcium, magnesium, phosphate, and bicarbonate.

They do real work. MedlinePlus says electrolytes help balance the water in your body, move nutrients into cells, and keep nerves and muscles working. They also help keep your heart rhythm steady and your blood pressure stable.

The same page states the source plainly: you get electrolytes from the foods you eat and the fluids you drink. A supplement is one more source. It is not the main one.

Do most people already get enough from food?

For sodium, most people get more than enough. The FDA says Americans eat about 3,400 mg of sodium per day on average. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans recommend that adults stay under 2,300 mg per day, which equals about 1 teaspoon of table salt.

The FDA adds that more than 70% of dietary sodium comes from packaged and prepared foods, not from the salt shaker. The CDC gives a similar picture: an average of more than 3,300 mg a day, against a guideline of less than 2,300 mg for teens and adults.

Sodium is the main electrolyte in most electrolyte drinks and powders. A person who already eats above the limit adds to that total with each serving. Amounts differ by product, so read the Nutrition Facts or Supplement Facts panel on the one you buy.

Potassium is a different story. MedlinePlus lists the adequate intake for adults as 2,600 mg a day for women and 3,400 mg a day for men. It names beans, lentils, potatoes with the skin, tomatoes, citrus fruits, bananas, milk, yogurt, and nuts as good sources. Food is the usual way to get it.

Magnesium is also an electrolyte, and it has its own evidence. See our comparison of magnesium supplements.

When do electrolyte drinks or supplements help?

They help when you lose more fluid and salt than normal eating and drinking replace. MedlinePlus lists these causes of an electrolyte imbalance:

  • severe vomiting or diarrhea
  • heavy sweating
  • not drinking enough during exercise or hot weather
  • drinking too much water
  • certain medicines
  • heart, liver, or kidney problems

You do not have to be an athlete to sweat heavily. A long shift outdoors in the heat can do it. The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) position stand on fluid replacement says the aim of drinking during exercise is to prevent a water deficit of more than 2% of body weight and large changes in electrolyte balance.

The ACSM wording is cautious. It says drinks with electrolytes and carbohydrates can provide benefits over water alone "under certain circumstances." It also notes that sweat rate and sweat electrolyte content vary a lot between people. A short walk or a desk day does not fit those circumstances.

For mild dehydration, MedlinePlus suggests sipping water, or drinking water or sports drinks that contain electrolytes. It also gives a warning: do not take salt tablets, because they can cause serious complications.

What about vomiting or diarrhea?

This is the clearest case for an electrolyte product, and the product matters. A CDC report on acute gastroenteritis in children recommends oral rehydration solution (ORS). ORS works because sodium and glucose are absorbed together in the gut. The report says this coupled transport keeps working even in severe diarrhea.

The same report says other fluids commonly used for diarrhea do not have sound concentrations of carbohydrates and electrolytes compared with ORS. It advises against large amounts of soft drinks, juice, and other highly sugared liquids, which can make diarrhea worse. The report dates from 2003 and covers children, so ask a clinician what applies to you.

MedlinePlus says severe dehydration may need fluids through a vein in a hospital. It says to call 911 if the person loses consciousness, becomes less alert, or has a fever over 102°F (38.8°C). Call too if the person does not improve with treatment.

Can drinking too much plain water be a problem?

Yes, but mainly during long events. Low blood sodium is called hyponatremia. A study of 488 runners in the 2002 Boston Marathon found that 13% finished with hyponatremia. In 0.6% it was critical.

The risk factors are the useful part. Hyponatremia was linked to weight gain during the race, a finish time over 4 hours, and very low or high body-mass index. Weight gain during a race is a sign of drinking more fluid than the body lost.

The type of fluid was not linked to hyponatremia in that study. In other words, a sports drink did not protect runners who drank too much. The study does not support adding electrolytes to water in daily life.

Who should be careful with electrolyte supplements?

Three groups have the most to lose from extra sodium or potassium.

  • High blood pressure. The FDA says a high sodium intake is associated with a higher risk of high blood pressure. MedlinePlus says adults with high blood pressure should have no more than 1,500 mg of sodium per day. Our sibling site hypertension.md covers sodium and blood pressure.
  • Kidney disease. The NIDDK says chronic kidney disease can make it harder for the kidneys to remove potassium, and blood levels can become very high. It also warns that salt substitutes can be high in potassium.
  • Heart failure. The NHLBI says people with heart failure may be asked to limit salt and liquids to reduce fluid buildup. MedlinePlus says too much sodium can cause a serious buildup of fluid in people with heart failure, cirrhosis, or kidney disease.

Medicines matter too. MedlinePlus says too much potassium in the blood can cause abnormal and dangerous heart rhythms. It lists ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and potassium-sparing diuretics such as spironolactone as common causes. If you take any of these, check our guide to supplements that interact with prescription drugs.

Electrolyte products also add to everything else you take. Our article on taking too many supplements explains how totals build up across products.

The bottom line

If you are healthy, eat regular meals, and do not sweat heavily for long periods, you do not need an electrolyte supplement. Water and food cover it, and most Americans already eat too much sodium. Electrolyte products have a place during prolonged heavy sweating and during vomiting or diarrhea, where oral rehydration solution is the tested option. If you have high blood pressure, kidney disease, or heart failure, or you take medicines that raise potassium, ask your clinician first.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do electrolyte drinks hydrate better than water for a normal day?

For a normal day with regular meals, water is enough for most healthy adults. The ACSM position stand says drinks with electrolytes and carbohydrates can beat water alone only under certain circumstances during exercise. A desk day or a short walk is not one of them.

Are salt tablets a good idea in hot weather?

No. MedlinePlus says not to take salt tablets for dehydration because they can cause serious complications. Drink water or a sports drink that contains electrolytes, and get medical help if symptoms do not improve.

Which electrolyte do Americans tend to get too much of?

Sodium. The FDA says Americans eat about 3,400 mg of sodium a day on average, while the Dietary Guidelines for Americans recommend less than 2,300 mg. More than 70% of that sodium comes from packaged and prepared foods.

Can I use a sports drink for a child with a stomach bug?

A CDC report on acute gastroenteritis in children recommends oral rehydration solution. It says other drinks commonly used for diarrhea do not have sound concentrations of carbohydrates and electrolytes. Ask your child's clinician which product to use.

Is potassium in electrolyte powders safe with blood pressure medicine?

It depends on the medicine. MedlinePlus lists ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and potassium-sparing diuretics as common causes of high blood potassium. Ask your clinician or pharmacist before you add a product that contains potassium.
