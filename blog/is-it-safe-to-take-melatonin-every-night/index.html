Is It Safe to Take Melatonin Every Night?

October 15, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Short-term melatonin use appears safe for most adults, according to the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH). Long-term nightly use is a different question: the safety data are thin, and one preliminary 2025 study linked a year or more of use to higher heart failure risk. Product doses are also unreliable, and the main sleep medicine guideline does not recommend melatonin for chronic insomnia.

What does the research say about taking melatonin every night?

Melatonin is a hormone your brain makes in response to darkness. It helps set the timing of your 24-hour internal clock. Supplements are usually made synthetically and are sold in tablets, capsules, liquids, and gummies.

In the United States, melatonin is a dietary supplement, not an FDA-approved drug. The NCCIH notes that this means it is regulated less strictly than prescription or over-the-counter medicines. In several other countries, melatonin is available only with a prescription. Our guide to how supplements are regulated explains what that difference means in practice.

Most of the research on melatonin covers short periods and specific timing problems. According to NCCIH, the best support is for:

  • Jet lag. Reviews from 2010 and 2014 suggest melatonin may reduce jet lag symptoms after eastward and westward flights.
  • Delayed sleep-wake phase disorder. In a 2018 trial of 307 people, melatonin taken 1 hour before the desired bedtime, plus a set bedtime, helped people fall asleep an average of 34 minutes earlier.
  • Anxiety before surgery. A 2015 review of 12 studies with 774 people found melatonin was better than placebo for anxiety before surgery.

None of these uses involves taking melatonin every night for years. That is the gap most nightly users fall into.

Is long-term melatonin use safe?

Nobody knows for sure. The NCCIH states that short-term use "appears to be safe for most people," but information on long-term safety "is lacking." It adds that there is not enough information on side effects at doses higher than what the body normally makes.

In November 2025, the American Heart Association publicized a preliminary study that looked at this question. Researchers reviewed 5 years of health records for 130,828 adults with insomnia. Key findings:

  • People with 12 months or more of recorded melatonin use had about a 90% higher chance of a new heart failure diagnosis (4.6% vs. 2.7%).
  • They were nearly 3.5 times as likely to be hospitalized for heart failure (19.0% vs. 6.6%).
  • They were nearly twice as likely to die from any cause over 5 years (7.8% vs. 4.3%).

This study has real limits. It was a conference abstract, not a peer-reviewed paper. It shows an association, not cause and effect. Melatonin use came only from health-record entries, so people who bought it over the counter in the U.S. likely ended up in the "non-user" group. The researchers also lacked data on insomnia severity and on depression or anxiety, which could affect both melatonin use and heart risk.

The fair reading: this is a signal that needs follow-up, not proof of harm. It does show that "natural" and "safe for years" are not the same claim.

Does the dose on the label match what is in the bottle?

Often it does not. Two lab studies found large gaps between label claims and real content.

A 2017 study in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine tested 31 melatonin supplements. Melatonin content ranged from 83% less to 478% more than the label stated. Lot-to-lot content within one product varied by as much as 465%. More than 71% of products missed their label claim by more than 10%. Eight supplements also contained serotonin, a related compound that the researchers note is used to treat some neurological disorders.

A 2023 research letter in JAMA measured melatonin in gummies sold in the U.S. As summarized by NCCIH, 22 of 25 gummy products were inaccurately labeled. One had no detectable melatonin. The rest held 74% to 347% of the labeled amount, and most held more than the label said.

For a nightly user, this means the real dose can change from bottle to bottle. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) suggests choosing a product with the USP Verified Mark. Our article on what third-party tested means covers how these seals work and what they do not promise.

Does melatonin actually work for chronic insomnia?

The evidence is weak. The 2017 AASM clinical practice guideline on drug treatment of chronic insomnia in adults suggests that clinicians not use melatonin for trouble falling asleep or staying asleep. That was a "weak" recommendation, which reflects limited evidence rather than proof that melatonin never helps.

The NCCIH adds that the American College of Physicians strongly recommends cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) as the first treatment. If you reach for melatonin every night because you cannot sleep, the underlying insomnia may be the real issue. Insomnia.md covers CBT-I and other evidence-based options in more depth.

Melatonin acts mainly on sleep timing. That explains why it shows its clearest effects in jet lag and delayed sleep phase, where the body clock is off schedule.

What side effects and interactions should you know about?

A 2015 safety review cited by NCCIH found only mild side effects in short-term adult studies. These included:

  • Headache
  • Dizziness
  • Nausea
  • Sleepiness

Some groups need more care. NCCIH says people with epilepsy and people taking blood thinners should use melatonin only under medical supervision. Melatonin may stay active longer in older adults and cause daytime drowsiness. The AASM's 2015 guidelines recommend against melatonin use by people with dementia. There is also little research on safety during pregnancy or breastfeeding.

If you take prescription medicines, check with your clinician or pharmacist before adding melatonin. Our overview of supplements that interact with prescription drugs explains why this matters.

Is melatonin safe for children?

Children are where the clearest safety problems have shown up, mostly from accidental ingestion. A 2022 CDC MMWR report counted 260,435 pediatric melatonin ingestions reported to U.S. poison control centers from 2012 to 2021. Annual ingestions rose 530% over that period. In 2020, melatonin became the substance children most often ingested in poison center reports.

Most of those exposed had no symptoms. But hospitalizations rose, five children needed mechanical ventilation, and two children died. NCCIH notes the data did not show whether melatonin caused those deaths.

The AASM health advisory on melatonin in children advises parents to:

  1. Treat melatonin like any other medicine and store it out of children's reach.
  2. Talk with a pediatric health care professional before starting it.
  3. Try changes in schedules, habits, or behaviors first, since many sleep problems respond to them.

NCCIH also notes that, because melatonin is a hormone, it could affect puberty or other hormonal development. That has not been proven, and long-term data in children are scarce.

The bottom line

Melatonin looks safe for most adults over short periods, and it has the best evidence for jet lag and body-clock timing problems. Nightly use for months or years has not been well studied, and a preliminary 2025 study raised a heart failure signal that still needs confirmation. Label doses are often wrong, so a third-party verified product makes more sense if you use it. If you need melatonin every night to sleep, talk with a clinician about the cause of your insomnia.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you become dependent on melatonin?

The sources reviewed here do not report dependence as a known effect of melatonin. The bigger concern is that nightly use can mask an untreated sleep problem such as chronic insomnia. Guidelines favor cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia as the first treatment.

Is melatonin regulated by the FDA?

In the United States, melatonin is sold as a dietary supplement, not as an FDA-approved drug. That means it does not go through drug approval before it reaches stores. In several other countries, including the United Kingdom, it is available only by prescription.

Are melatonin gummies less accurate than tablets?

A 2023 JAMA study found 22 of 25 melatonin gummy products were mislabeled, with some holding up to about 3.5 times the stated amount. A 2017 study of 31 supplements found the widest variation in chewable tablets. Gummies also appeal to children, which raises the risk of accidental ingestion.

Is it safe to take melatonin while pregnant?

There is little research on melatonin safety in pregnancy or breastfeeding, according to the NCCIH. Because the data are lacking, talk with your prenatal care provider before using it.

Does melatonin interact with any medicines?

The NCCIH says people with epilepsy and people taking blood thinners should use melatonin only under medical supervision. Anyone on prescription medicines should check with a clinician or pharmacist before starting it.
