Is It Better To Take Iron Every Other Day Instead Of Daily?

September 19, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida
Table of Contents

The short answer: Taking iron every other day does increase how much of each dose your body absorbs, and it appears to cause fewer stomach side effects. It does not clearly raise hemoglobin any faster, and the blood count evidence is much thinner than the absorption evidence. No major society has made it the standard first choice, so the schedule is your clinician's call.

Why would taking iron every other day work at all?

The idea rests on a hormone called hepcidin. Made in the liver, it blocks iron from moving out of gut cells into the blood. An oral iron dose raises hepcidin, so the next dose meets a closed door.

Researchers measured how long that door stays shut. In a 2015 study in Blood, 54 young women with low iron stores (plasma ferritin 20 micrograms per liter or less) took labeled iron doses of 40 to 240 mg. Twenty-four hours after doses of 60 mg or more, serum hepcidin was still raised and fractional iron absorption had fallen by 35% to 45%.

A later crossover study in Haematologica tested 19 women who already had iron deficiency anemia (median hemoglobin 11.5 g/dL, mean serum ferritin 10 micrograms per liter). Even in anemia, where hepcidin is normally suppressed, a 100 mg or 200 mg dose still raised hepcidin for about 24 hours. Fractional absorption on a skipped day was 40% to 50% higher than on the second of two consecutive days.

Does it mean more iron absorbed, or only better efficiency per dose?

These are two different claims. The clearest test is a pair of open-label randomised trials published in The Lancet Haematology in 2017. Forty iron-depleted Swiss women (serum ferritin 25 micrograms per liter or less) took 60 mg of iron at 8 AM. One group took it on consecutive days for 14 days. The other took the same 14 doses on alternate days across 28 days.

  • Cumulative fractional absorption: 16.3% on consecutive days versus 21.8% on alternate days.
  • Cumulative total iron absorbed: 131.0 mg versus 175.3 mg.
  • Serum hepcidin during the first 14 days was higher in the consecutive-day group.

So both efficiency and total absorbed iron went up. Read the design closely, though. The alternate-day group got the same number of doses, but took twice as many calendar days. Efficiency per dose is the finding that generalizes. Absorbing more iron in a fixed number of weeks is not what that trial showed.

The Haematologica study adds a wrinkle. Total iron absorbed from one 200 mg dose on alternate days was roughly double that from 100 mg on consecutive days. Doubling the dose and halving the frequency, not simply skipping days, is what kept the total up.

One limit is worth stating plainly. These are small isotope studies in iron-depleted young women in Switzerland. They measure iron entering red blood cells, not how fast anemia resolves.

Does alternate-day dosing raise hemoglobin any faster?

Here the evidence gets weaker, and it does not favor alternate-day dosing.

A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 150 women compared 100 mg of iron daily for 90 days against the same dose on alternate days for 180 days. At equal total iron, median serum ferritin was 43.8 versus 44.8 micrograms per liter. Iron deficiency at six months was 11.4% in the daily group and 3.0% in the alternate-day group.

In actual anemia, a randomized controlled trial of 200 adults with hemoglobin of 10 g/dL or less compared 120 mg on alternate days against 60 mg daily for eight weeks. Mean hemoglobin rose 1.05 g/dL on alternate days and 1.36 g/dL daily. That difference was not statistically significant, and no secondary outcome separated the groups either.

A one-month Turkish trial in women with iron deficiency anemia found similar hemoglobin gains across daily and every-other-day arms. A 2026 pilot trial in 60 iron-deficient Indian women pointed the other way over 14 days: daily 60 mg produced larger gains in mean corpuscular volume and mean corpuscular hemoglobin than 120 mg on alternate days.

The honest summary: absorption studies favor alternate-day dosing, hemoglobin studies mostly show a tie, and one small trial favors daily.

Does every-other-day dosing cause fewer stomach problems?

This has been measured, not assumed. In the 150-woman placebo-controlled trial, participants logged side effects daily in an app, with reporting above 97%. On days when iron was taken, gastrointestinal side effects were 1.56 times as likely in the consecutive-day group (95% CI 1.38 to 1.77).

The Turkish trial found more gastrointestinal complaints in the arm taking 160 mg of elemental iron per day than in the once-daily or alternate-day arms. That arm also took twice as much iron, so dose and schedule are tangled. The 200-person anemia trial found no significant difference in adverse events between schedules.

There is also a mechanical reason for fewer symptoms: you swallow iron on half as many days. Amount and frequency both matter, a theme across supplement dosing generally.

Do any medical guidelines recommend alternate-day iron?

Not as the default. The 2021 British Society of Gastroenterology guideline on iron deficiency anaemia in adults reviewed this literature. It concluded that a once-daily dose of 50 to 100 mg of elemental iron, taken fasting, "may be the best compromise option for initial treatment."

It also states that the effect on adherence and final blood response is unclear. It lists alternate-day oral iron as one option for people who cannot tolerate standard oral iron, alongside ferric maltol and intravenous iron.

The World Health Organization does recommend an intermittent schedule, for a different purpose. Its guideline on intermittent iron and folic acid supplementation in menstruating women supports 60 mg of elemental iron plus 2.8 mg of folic acid once weekly for three months, then three months off. That is population-level prevention where anemia prevalence is 20% or higher, not a treatment plan for a diagnosed individual.

What else changes how much iron you absorb?

Splitting a dose within one day is a separate question, and the answer is clearer. In the Lancet Haematology trials, 120 mg taken once at 8 AM and 60 mg taken twice gave similar total absorption (44.3 mg versus 49.4 mg over three days), but the split schedule produced higher hepcidin. The 2015 Blood study found that adding an afternoon dose to two morning doses did not significantly increase total iron absorbed.

Other documented modifiers, drawn from the National Academies iron chapter and the BSG guideline:

  • Food. Taking iron salts with meals can cut bioavailability by up to 75%.
  • Calcium. At 300 to 600 mg in a meal, absorption drops by 50% to 60%, though long-term calcium supplements show little effect on iron stores.
  • Tea and coffee polyphenols. These markedly inhibit non-heme iron absorption. The BSG notes the inhibitory effect of tea fades within 60 minutes.
  • Proton pump inhibitors. Long-term use is linked to higher iron deficiency risk, probably through reduced stomach acid.
  • Vitamin C. In the laboratory it strongly boosts non-heme iron absorption. In a 440-patient randomized trial, adding 200 mg of vitamin C to iron gave equivalent hemoglobin recovery to iron alone.

If you take other medicines, spacing matters as much as the schedule. See our guides to supplement timing and supplement and drug interactions.

The bottom line

Alternate-day iron is a real finding with a real mechanism, and it is oversold. Per dose, you absorb more. Over a treatment course, hemoglobin recovery looks about the same, and the side effect advantage is the most practical reason to consider it.

None of this is a reason to start iron on your own. Tiredness has many causes, as our sister site covers in its guide to fatigue. Iron deficiency has an underlying cause that needs finding, which is why guidelines pair treatment with investigation. Too much iron is harmful: MedlinePlus notes that excess intake causes constipation, nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain, and that extremely high levels can cause organ damage. The National Academies set the adult tolerable upper intake level at 45 mg per day, well above the 18 mg daily allowance for premenopausal women. Treatment doses sit far above that, which is why a clinician sets the dose and the duration.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does one iron dose keep blocking the next one?

About 24 hours. In the 2015 Blood study, doses of 60 mg or more still showed raised serum hepcidin and a 35% to 45% drop in fractional absorption a full day later. The same 24-hour pattern appeared in women who already had iron deficiency anemia.

If I switch to every other day, should I take a bigger tablet?

That is a question for your clinician, not a self-adjustment. The Haematologica crossover study found that a single 200 mg dose on alternate days delivered roughly twice the absorbed iron of 100 mg on consecutive days. Simply skipping days without changing the dose halves the total iron you take in.

Do I need to take vitamin C with my iron?

Probably not. Vitamin C is a strong enhancer of non-heme iron absorption in laboratory conditions. But a randomized trial in 440 adults with iron deficiency anemia found that iron alone matched iron plus 200 mg of vitamin C for hemoglobin recovery and ferritin gain.

How quickly should an iron supplement show results in blood tests?

The British Society of Gastroenterology cites pooled trial data in which 72.8% of patients with iron deficiency anemia had a satisfactory response, defined as a hemoglobin rise of more than 10 g/L within two weeks. Response rates were lower with ongoing bleeding or gut disease. Monitoring the early response is part of the guideline.

Is slow-release iron a better way to avoid side effects?

The BSG guideline advises against modified-release preparations. They release iron further down the small bowel, past the area where absorption is most active. The guideline states they neither improve absorption nor reduce side effects.
