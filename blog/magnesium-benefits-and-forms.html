Magnesium: Benefits, Forms, and How to Choose the Right One

February 2026

Magnesium is having a moment. Browse any supplement aisle or wellness feed and you will encounter magnesium glycinate for sleep, magnesium L-threonate for brain health, magnesium citrate for regularity, and magnesium taurate for the heart. The marketing suggests that each form does something fundamentally different in the body. The reality is more nuanced, but the underlying premise -- that many people do not get enough magnesium -- is supported by the data.

Why Magnesium Matters

Magnesium is a cofactor in more than 300 enzymatic reactions, including protein synthesis, muscle and nerve function, blood glucose regulation, and blood pressure control. It is required for energy production (ATP synthesis), DNA and RNA synthesis, and the structural development of bone.

About 60% of the body's magnesium resides in bone, 39% in soft tissue, and less than 1% circulates in blood. This last point is important: standard serum magnesium tests measure only what is in the blood, which is a poor reflection of total body stores. You can have a "normal" serum magnesium level and still be functionally depleted in tissues.

How Common Is Deficiency?

Analysis of NHANES data indicates that roughly 48% of Americans consume less than the Estimated Average Requirement for magnesium from food alone. This does not mean half the population is clinically deficient, but it does suggest widespread suboptimal intake.

The RDA for magnesium is 400-420 mg daily for adult men and 310-320 mg for adult women. Foods rich in magnesium include dark leafy greens, nuts and seeds (pumpkin seeds are particularly dense), legumes, whole grains, and dark chocolate. A single ounce of pumpkin seeds provides about 156 mg. A cup of cooked spinach delivers around 157 mg.

Factors that increase the risk of true deficiency include:

  • Chronic alcohol use, which increases renal excretion
  • Type 2 diabetes, where insulin resistance promotes urinary magnesium loss
  • Gastrointestinal diseases (Crohn's, celiac, chronic diarrhea)
  • Long-term proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use, which impairs absorption
  • Older age, due to both decreased intake and decreased absorption
  • Diuretic medications, particularly loop and thiazide diuretics

The Forms: What Is Actually Different

Magnesium supplements consist of elemental magnesium bound to a carrier molecule. The carrier affects two things that matter clinically: bioavailability (how much elemental magnesium you actually absorb) and side effects (mainly GI tolerance). A practical observation: the differences between well-absorbed forms (citrate, glycinate, malate) are smaller than supplement marketing suggests. If your goal is to correct a general dietary shortfall, any of these will work. The choice often comes down to GI tolerance and cost.

A Word on Magnesium L-Threonate

This form has attracted significant attention since a 2010 Neuron paper by Bhatt et al. showed that magnesium L-threonate increased brain magnesium levels in rats more effectively than other forms, with corresponding improvements in learning and memory. However, human clinical data remains limited to a small number of studies, most with methodological limitations. It is substantially more expensive than other forms. The marketing has far outpaced the evidence.

What the Research Supports

Blood Pressure

A 2016 meta-analysis in Hypertension (34 randomized controlled trials, 2,028 participants) found that magnesium supplementation at a median dose of 368 mg/day reduced systolic blood pressure by 2.0 mmHg and diastolic by 1.78 mmHg. The effect was modest but consistent, and was more pronounced in people with existing deficiency.

Type 2 Diabetes Risk

A meta-analysis published in the Journal of Internal Medicine found that each 100 mg/day increase in magnesium intake was associated with a 14% reduction in type 2 diabetes risk. This is observational data, so causation is not established, but the biological mechanism (magnesium's role in insulin signaling) is plausible.

Migraine Prevention

The American Academy of Neurology and the American Headache Society list magnesium (400-600 mg daily) as "probably effective" for migraine prevention. A randomized trial published in Cephalalgia found that 600 mg of magnesium citrate daily reduced migraine frequency by 41.6% compared to 15.8% with placebo over 12 weeks.

Sleep

The evidence for magnesium and sleep is modest but suggestive. A 2012 randomized trial in the Journal of Research in Medical Sciences found that 500 mg of magnesium daily (as magnesium oxide, notably the least bioavailable form) improved subjective sleep quality in elderly participants with insomnia. The effect was attributed to magnesium's role in regulating GABA receptors and melatonin production. Larger, better-designed trials are needed.

Muscle Cramps

Despite being one of the most commonly cited reasons for taking magnesium, the evidence for treating muscle cramps is surprisingly weak. A 2020 Cochrane review found no significant benefit of magnesium supplementation for general muscle cramps. There may be benefit in pregnancy-related leg cramps, but the data is inconsistent.

Who Should Be Cautious

People with kidney disease should not supplement magnesium without medical supervision, as impaired kidneys cannot efficiently excrete excess magnesium. High-dose magnesium can also interact with certain antibiotics (tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones), bisphosphonates, and some blood pressure medications. If you take prescription drugs, check with your pharmacist before starting supplementation.

Practical Recommendations

If dietary analysis or clinical suspicion suggests inadequate magnesium intake, supplementation is reasonable. Here is a sensible approach:

  1. Start with food. A handful of pumpkin seeds, a serving of dark leafy greens, or a portion of black beans can close much of the gap.
  2. Choose a well-absorbed form. Magnesium citrate or glycinate are the best balance of evidence, absorption, tolerance, and cost.
  3. Start at 200 mg of elemental magnesium and increase to 400 mg if tolerated. Take with food to improve absorption and reduce GI effects.
  4. Split doses if needed. Taking 200 mg twice daily rather than 400 mg at once improves absorption and reduces the chance of loose stools.
  5. Be patient. It can take 4-6 weeks of consistent supplementation to replenish tissue stores.

The Bottom Line

Magnesium is one of the few supplements where the gap between typical intake and recommended intake is well-documented. If you eat a diet low in nuts, seeds, whole grains, and leafy greens, supplementation is a reasonable insurance policy. Pick a bioavailable form, keep the dose moderate, and do not expect miracles from any particular branded version. The mineral itself is what matters.
