Do Collagen Supplements Actually Work?

August 30, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida
Table of Contents

The short answer: Partly, and less than the marketing says. Pooled trials show small gains in skin hydration and elasticity, but a 2025 meta-analysis found those gains vanish in studies the collagen industry did not fund. The evidence is stronger for knee osteoarthritis pain, bone density in postmenopausal women, and muscle gains when collagen is paired with strength training. Most of those trials were also small, short, or industry-funded.

Does collagen improve skin hydration, elasticity, and wrinkles?

On paper, yes. A 2023 meta-analysis in Nutrients pooled 26 randomized controlled trials with 1,721 participants. Hydrolyzed collagen improved skin hydration (effect size 0.63, 95% CI 0.38 to 0.88) and elasticity (0.72, 95% CI 0.40 to 1.03). Trials used doses from under 1 g to 10 g per day, for 2 to 12 weeks. Trials that ran longer than 8 weeks showed larger effects.

An earlier 2021 review in the International Journal of Dermatology reached the same conclusion. It covered 19 trials and 1,125 participants, aged 20 to 70, and 95% of them were women. The authors concluded that 90 days of hydrolyzed collagen reduced wrinkles and improved elasticity and hydration.

Those effect sizes are moderate, not dramatic. They come from instrument readings of skin moisture and stretch, not from changes you would notice in a mirror. None of the trials followed people for years.

Who paid for the skin studies, and does it matter?

This is the biggest problem with collagen research. In 2025, Myung and Park published a meta-analysis in The American Journal of Medicine that sorted 23 trials with 1,474 participants by funding source and study quality. Across all 23 trials, collagen improved hydration, elasticity, and wrinkles. Then they split the data.

  • Trials with no pharmaceutical or supplement industry funding showed no effect on hydration, elasticity, or wrinkles.
  • High-quality trials showed no significant effect in any category.
  • Low-quality trials showed an improvement in elasticity only.

Their conclusion: "There is currently no clinical evidence to support the use of collagen supplements to prevent or treat skin aging." That does not prove collagen fails. It means the positive results depend on who ran the trial. Until independent groups reproduce the skin findings, treat them as unproven. Does collagen help joint pain and osteoarthritis?

The joint data is thinner but more consistent. A 2023 meta-analysis in the Journal of Orthopaedic Surgery and Research found 4 trials with 507 knee osteoarthritis patients. Collagen peptides reduced pain more than placebo (standardized mean difference −0.58, 95% CI −0.98 to −0.18, p = 0.004). The trials used 8 to 10 g per day for 3 to 6 months. The authors rated the evidence as moderate quality. All four trials had a high risk of bias.

The best-known trial in healthy people is older. In 2008, Penn State researchers randomized 147 varsity and club athletes with activity-related joint pain to 10 g of collagen hydrolysate or placebo for 24 weeks. Only 97 could be analyzed. Physician-rated joint pain at rest fell 1.37 points on the collagen side versus 0.90 on placebo (p = 0.025). Pain when walking, standing, carrying, and lifting also improved more with collagen. The differences were small, and one third of participants dropped out.

Side effects were mild. In the osteoarthritis meta-analysis, the most common complaints were stomach upset, headache, and respiratory infections, at rates not significantly different from placebo.

Can collagen increase bone density?

One well-designed trial says yes, and the effect was measurable. In 2018, König and colleagues enrolled 131 postmenopausal women (mean age 64.3) with reduced bone density. They took 5 g of specific collagen peptides or 5 g of maltodextrin placebo every day for 12 months. In the collagen group, the spine T-score moved from −2.54 to −2.47 (p = 0.021). The femoral neck T-score moved from −1.41 to −1.32 (p = 0.002). The placebo group did not change. A bone formation marker, P1NP, also rose only in the collagen group.

Two caveats. Only 102 of the 131 women finished the study. And the paper states that "part of the study was financially supported by GELITA AG," a collagen manufacturer, with one author affiliated with the Collagen Research Institute. A 2025 meta-analysis in Frontiers in Nutrition pooled 17 randomized trials on bone and muscle and found consistent gains in bone markers, but the bone density results varied widely between trials (I² = 80.1%). Collagen has not been shown to prevent fractures.

Does collagen build muscle?

Only with training, and the gains are modest. A 2024 meta-analysis in Sports Medicine pooled 19 trials with 768 participants who took collagen peptides during a physical training program. Fat-free mass rose (SMD 0.48, 95% CI 0.22 to 0.74) with moderate certainty. Maximal strength rose less (SMD 0.19, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.31) with low certainty. Muscle soreness did not change. Daily doses ranged from 3 to 30 g, and 15 g was the most common. The review received no external funding.

The trial that started this line of research came from the same Freiburg group as the bone study. In 2015 they gave 53 men with sarcopenia (mean age 72) 15 g of collagen peptides or placebo during 12 weeks of resistance training. Fat-free mass rose 4.2 kg with collagen versus 2.9 kg with placebo. Leg strength rose 16.5 Nm versus 7.3 Nm. Gelita AG paid part of the costs.

Collagen contains about 33% glycine, 10% proline, and 13.5% hydroxyproline, and no tryptophan. It is a low-quality protein for building muscle compared with whey or a full meal. If muscle is the goal, creatine and adequate total protein have far stronger evidence.

What happens to collagen after you swallow it?

A common claim is that collagen cannot work because the gut digests it into amino acids. That claim is only half right. Some of it survives as short peptides. A 2024 crossover study gave 6 healthy adults 10 g of collagen hydrolysate. Free hydroxyproline in blood rose 6 to 10 times above baseline within 100 to 130 minutes. The dipeptide prolyl-hydroxyproline (Pro-Hyp) was the most abundant peptide detected, peaking at 3.8 μg/mL from the porcine product. These peptides circulate, but whether they trigger new collagen synthesis in skin or cartilage in humans is unproven. A 2022 narrative review found no data to support the claim that collagen peptides stimulate connective tissue protein synthesis in people.

Hydrolyzed collagen and gelatin are the same protein at different sizes. Gelatin is made by heating collagen to 50 to 100°C, which unwinds the triple helix into strands of about 100 kDa. Hydrolyzed collagen (collagen peptides) is broken down further with enzymes or acids into fragments of 2 to 20 kDa, usually under 6 kDa. Both deliver similar peak amino acid levels in blood. Nearly all skin, joint, bone, and muscle trials used the hydrolyzed form.

Collagen is a dietary supplement, so the FDA does not review it for safety or effectiveness before sale. The manufacturer is responsible for safety and label claims, and the FDA acts only after a product reaches the market. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements publishes fact sheets for vitamins and minerals, but for collagen it offers only a glossary entry. Our roundup of supplements that actually work ranks collagen against the ones with independent evidence. If your goal is healthier aging overall, sleep, protein intake, and resistance exercise have more support; thrive.md covers those basics.

The bottom line

Collagen is not a scam, but it is oversold. Skin benefits show up in pooled trials and disappear in independent ones. Knee osteoarthritis pain and bone density in postmenopausal women have the best single-trial support, at 5 to 10 g per day for 3 to 12 months in the studies. Muscle gains require strength training and are smaller than what creatine or whey deliver. The peptides are absorbed, but nobody has shown they rebuild human tissue. If you try collagen, judge it the way the trials did: give it 12 weeks and expect a modest change.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does collagen take to show any effect?

Skin trials ran 2 to 12 weeks, and pooled results were better after 8 weeks. Joint trials ran 3 to 6 months, and the bone density trial ran 12 months. If you see no change after 12 weeks, the trials suggest you are unlikely to see one later.

Is marine collagen better than bovine collagen?

No trial has shown one source beats another for skin, joints, or bone. Both are hydrolyzed into similar short peptides. One absorption study found the fish-derived product gave higher levels of one dipeptide, but that has not been linked to a better clinical result.

Can I get the same benefit from bone broth or gelatin?

Gelatin is the same protein in larger pieces, and it raises blood amino acid levels about as much as hydrolyzed collagen. Bone broth has not been tested in the trials, and its collagen content varies widely. Nearly all positive trials used hydrolyzed collagen peptides in measured doses.

Does vitamin C need to be taken with collagen?

Vitamin C is required for the body to make its own collagen, but most trials did not add it. Several products include it anyway. There is no trial showing the combination outperforms collagen alone.

Are collagen supplements safe?

Trials report mild effects such as stomach upset, bloating, and headache, at rates similar to placebo. Collagen is made from cow, pig, fish, or chicken tissue, so allergies are possible. People with kidney disease should ask a clinician before adding any protein supplement.
