Can Biotin Supplements Throw Off Blood Test Results?

September 22, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida
Table of Contents

The short answer: Yes. High-dose biotin, the kind sold for hair, skin, and nails, can make some lab tests read falsely high or falsely low. The FDA has warned that it can cause a falsely low troponin result, which could hide a heart attack. Thyroid tests are another common target. Tell your clinician and the lab if you take biotin, because nobody can see it in your blood sample.

How can a vitamin change a lab result?

Biotin does not change your hormones or your heart. It changes the test. Many automated lab tests (immunoassays) use biotin as a molecular hook. The test attaches biotin to an antibody, and a protein called streptavidin grabs that biotin to hold the reaction in place.

If your blood already carries a lot of free biotin, it fills the streptavidin before the test's own biotin can bind. The machine then reads the wrong signal. Which way the result goes depends on the test design, according to FDA guidance to test makers:

  • Sandwich tests (used for larger molecules such as TSH and troponin) tend to read falsely low.
  • Competitive tests (used for small molecules such as free T4 and T3) tend to read falsely high.

The American Thyroid Association puts it plainly: biotin has no effect on thyroid function in the body. It interferes with the measurement in the lab.

Which blood tests can biotin throw off?

The FDA's November 5, 2019 safety communication says many tests that use biotin technology may be affected, "including but not limited to" cardiovascular tests and hormone tests. It singles out troponin, the main blood marker for a heart attack.

A small 2017 study in JAMA shows how broad the problem can be. Six healthy adults took 10 mg of biotin a day for 7 days. Researchers then ran 37 immunoassays for 11 analytes on 4 lab systems. The analytes included TSH, T4, T3, parathyroid hormone, prolactin, NT-proBNP (a heart failure marker), vitamin D, PSA, and ferritin.

  • Interference showed up in 9 of 23 assays (39%) that used biotin and streptavidin.
  • None of the 14 assays without biotin components were affected.
  • 5 of 8 competitive assays read falsely high. 4 of 15 sandwich assays read falsely low.

The key point is "some but not all." The same analyte can be fine on one lab machine and wrong on another. You cannot know which one your lab uses unless you ask.

Why does the FDA single out heart attack tests?

A falsely low troponin can make a real heart attack look like nothing. The FDA first warned about biotin on November 28, 2017, then updated the warning in 2019. The 2019 update states that before the first warning, the FDA received a report of one patient taking high levels of biotin who died after low troponin results from a test known to be subject to biotin interference.

The agency also said it keeps receiving adverse event reports of falsely low troponin tied to biotin. Some manufacturers have fixed their assays since 2017, but not all. The FDA keeps a public list of troponin assays where the risk had not yet been addressed.

Can biotin make thyroid tests look like Graves' disease?

Yes, and endocrinologists have documented it. According to the American Thyroid Association, biotin most commonly produces falsely high T4 and T3 with a falsely low TSH. That combination is the classic lab picture of an overactive thyroid.

A 2016 case report and literature review in the Journal of Clinical Endocrinology and Metabolism titled it "factitious Graves' disease." The patient's thyroid results were markedly abnormal and did not match the clinical picture. After biotin was stopped, the results normalized faster than T4's half-life would allow, which pointed to the assay, not the gland.

The 2026 European Thyroid Association guideline on assay interference adds that biotin can falsely raise TSH receptor antibodies, the marker used to confirm Graves' disease. It can also affect thyroglobulin, which people treated for thyroid cancer rely on for follow-up. The guideline tells clinicians to review supplements, "especially biotin," whenever thyroid results do not fit the patient.

A 2021 prospective study in the journal Thyroid gave 13 adults 10 mg of biotin a day for eight days. On one widely used platform, TSH, free T4, and total T3 changed significantly. Thyroglobulin read falsely low on another platform. Interference peaked about two hours after a dose. For more on what each thyroid number means, see thyroid.md's guide to thyroid blood tests explained.

How much biotin does it take to cause a problem?

The dose matters a lot. The FDA's 2019 communication notes the daily recommended amount for adults is 0.03 mg (30 mcg), and that amount does not typically cause interference.

Supplements are a different story. The FDA says:

  • Many supplements promoted for hair, skin, and nails contain up to 650 times the recommended daily intake.
  • It is aware of many supplements with 20 mg of biotin, and some with up to 100 mg per pill.
  • Physicians may recommend up to 300 mg a day for conditions such as multiple sclerosis.

The ATA notes that multivitamins typically contain 30 to 300 mcg, while hair and nail products usually contain 5,000 to 10,000 mcg (5 to 10 mg). Both study doses cited above were 10 mg a day, well within the range of common hair and nail products.

The FDA also points out that biotin hides in products whose names do not mention it. Multivitamins and prenatal vitamins can contain it too, so check the Supplement Facts panel. If you take several products, the amounts add up, a problem covered in our look at whether you can take too many supplements.

How long should you stop biotin before a blood test?

Here the sources do not agree, and one says the evidence is not there yet.

  • The FDA says sufficient information is not available to know whether stopping biotin for any number of hours before testing will prevent wrong results. It adds that data on how long biotin takes to clear the blood are insufficient.
  • The ATA's thyroid function test page says biotin should not be taken for 2 days before blood is drawn for thyroid testing.
  • An ATA patient summary from 2022 says patients taking biotin should generally stop for at least 3 to 5 days before thyroid tests.

The ETA guideline supports withholding biotin before testing and repeating a suspicious result after the supplement is stopped. It also notes that many manufacturers have redesigned assays to reduce biotin interference. Your lab may already use a resistant test, or it may not.

Two practical points follow. Do not stop a biotin dose a doctor prescribed without asking them first. And in an emergency, such as chest pain, there is no time to wait, so say you take biotin right away. The same habit of telling your care team about every supplement applies to supplements that interact with prescription drugs.

The bottom line

Biotin at the doses sold for hair, skin, and nails can make some lab tests wrong without anyone noticing. Troponin and thyroid tests carry the highest stakes. The fix is simple: tell your clinician and the person drawing your blood that you take biotin, and how much. If a result does not fit how you feel, ask whether biotin could explain it. Ask your clinician or lab how long to pause before planned tests, since published advice ranges from 2 days to "not enough data to say."

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the small amount of biotin in a multivitamin affect lab tests?

The FDA says the daily recommended amount of 0.03 mg does not typically cause interference. The ATA notes most multivitamins contain 30 to 300 mcg, far below hair and nail products. Amounts vary by product, so it is still worth mentioning any multivitamin to the lab.

Is interference worse right after I take a biotin dose?

It can be. In a 2021 study of 13 adults taking 10 mg a day, interference was greatest about two hours after a dose, the peak among the time points tested. Timing your blood draw is not a reliable fix, though, and the FDA says data on biotin clearance are insufficient.

Does biotin actually change my thyroid function?

No. The American Thyroid Association says biotin has no effect on thyroid hormone levels or thyroid function in the body. It only interferes with how some lab machines measure those hormones.

Can the lab tell if my sample has biotin in it?

Usually not. The FDA says it is difficult for labs to identify samples that contain biotin. That is why the agency asks patients and clinicians to tell the lab directly.

What if I take biotin because my doctor prescribed it?

Do not stop it on your own. The FDA notes physicians may recommend up to 300 mg a day for conditions such as multiple sclerosis, a dose far above supplement levels. Ask your doctor how to handle planned blood tests and make sure every lab knows about it.
