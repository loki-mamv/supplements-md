FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Evidence-based answers to common questions about dietary supplements, quality, safety, and effectiveness.

Regulation & Quality

Are supplements regulated by the FDA?

Yes, but differently from prescription drugs. Under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA) of 1994:

  • Supplements do not require FDA pre-approval before going to market
  • Manufacturers are responsible for ensuring their products are safe and labels are accurate
  • The FDA can take action against unsafe products after they're on the market
  • The FDA enforces Good Manufacturing Practices (GMPs) for supplement facilities

This is why third-party testing (USP, NSF) is so important—it provides independent verification that regulatory oversight alone doesn't guarantee.

What does USP Verified mean?

USP (United States Pharmacopeia) Verified is the gold standard for supplement quality. A USP Verified mark means the product has been independently tested and verified for:

  • Identity: Contains what the label claims
  • Potency: Contains the declared amounts
  • Purity: Free from harmful contaminants
  • Dissolution: Will break down properly in the body
  • Manufacturing: Made in a facility following Good Manufacturing Practices

Fewer than 1% of supplements carry this mark because the testing is expensive and rigorous. If available for your supplement, USP Verified is the best choice.

What's the difference between USP, NSF, and ConsumerLab?

All three provide valuable independent testing, with some differences:

  • USP Verified: Most comprehensive. Tests identity, potency, purity, dissolution, and manufacturing. Includes ongoing facility audits. Gold standard.
  • NSF Certified for Sport: Tests for label accuracy, contaminants, and 280+ banned substances. Required by many sports organizations. Great for athletes.
  • ConsumerLab Approved: Tests products for label accuracy and contaminants. Tests products without manufacturer participation. Good for comparing brands.

Any of these certifications is better than no third-party testing. Choose based on availability and your specific needs.

Is "pharmaceutical grade" meaningful?

Not really. "Pharmaceutical grade" is a marketing term with no legal definition for dietary supplements. It might suggest the raw ingredients meet USP standards, but it doesn't guarantee:

  • The finished product was tested
  • The amounts on the label are accurate
  • Manufacturing was independently verified

Look for actual third-party certifications (USP Verified, NSF for Sport) rather than marketing buzzwords.

Safety & Interactions

Can supplements interact with my medications?

Yes, many supplements can interact with prescription and over-the-counter medications. Some important examples:

  • St. John's Wort: Reduces effectiveness of birth control, HIV medications, blood thinners, and many other drugs
  • Vitamin K: Reduces warfarin's anticoagulant effect
  • Calcium/Iron: Reduces absorption of thyroid medications and antibiotics (take 4+ hours apart)
  • Fish oil/Vitamin E: May increase bleeding risk with blood thinners
  • Grapefruit: Increases blood levels of many drugs to potentially toxic amounts

Always tell your healthcare provider about ALL supplements you take. Bring the bottles to appointments.

Is "natural" safer than synthetic?

No. "Natural" doesn't mean safe, effective, or superior to synthetic alternatives:

  • Many natural substances are toxic (arsenic, cyanide, hemlock, certain mushrooms)
  • Synthetic vitamins are often chemically identical to natural forms
  • "Natural" has no regulatory definition—it's essentially a marketing term
  • Some natural products have more contamination issues than synthetic

Evaluate supplements based on evidence, quality testing, and safety data—not whether they're labeled natural or synthetic.

Should I stop supplements before surgery?

Yes, many supplements should be stopped 1-2 weeks before surgery due to bleeding risk or anesthesia interactions. Commonly stopped supplements include:

  • Fish oil / omega-3s
  • Vitamin E (high dose)
  • Ginkgo biloba, garlic, ginger, ginseng
  • St. John's Wort
  • Kava and valerian

Bring a complete list of all supplements to your pre-operative appointment. Your surgical team needs this information.

Can I take too much of a vitamin?

Yes. "More is better" doesn't apply to vitamins and minerals. Excess amounts can be harmful:

  • Vitamin A: Toxicity causes liver damage, bone problems, birth defects
  • Vitamin D: Excess causes hypercalcemia (high blood calcium)
  • Vitamin E: High doses may increase bleeding and mortality risk
  • Iron: Toxic in excess; can be fatal in children
  • Selenium: Toxicity causes hair loss, nail problems, nerve damage

Stick to recommended doses unless directed otherwise by a healthcare provider treating a documented deficiency.

Evidence & Effectiveness

Do I need to take a multivitamin?

Most healthy adults eating a varied diet don't need a multivitamin. The evidence:

  • Large clinical trials show no significant reduction in heart disease, cancer, or mortality
  • The US Preventive Services Task Force does not recommend multivitamins for disease prevention
  • COSMOS-Mind trial showed a small cognitive benefit in older adults (needs replication)

People who may benefit include those with documented deficiencies, pregnant women (prenatal vitamins), older adults with poor absorption, and those with very restrictive diets.

Will vitamin C prevent colds?

No. Despite popular belief, vitamin C does not prevent colds in most people. The evidence:

  • Regular supplementation doesn't reduce the number of colds you get
  • It may shorten cold duration by about half a day (modest benefit)
  • This benefit only occurs if you're already taking it before getting sick—not after
  • People under extreme physical stress (marathon runners) may have more benefit

Most people get adequate vitamin C from diet. Supplements are unlikely to provide additional immune benefit.

Does biotin help hair and nails?

Probably not, unless you're biotin deficient (which is rare). The evidence:

  • No clinical trials show biotin helps hair growth in people with normal biotin levels
  • One small study suggested benefit for brittle nails, but needs replication
  • Biotin deficiency is uncommon; symptoms include thinning hair, scaly rash, and neurological issues

Important warning: High-dose biotin can interfere with many lab tests, including thyroid and cardiac tests. This can lead to misdiagnosis. Stop biotin at least 2 days (ideally 1 week) before lab work.

Should I take vitamin D?

It depends on your vitamin D level. Vitamin D has strong evidence for bone health and deficiency treatment, but is often overhyped for other conditions.

Consider testing if you:

  • Have limited sun exposure
  • Have dark skin
  • Are over 65
  • Have osteoporosis or bone issues
  • Have malabsorption conditions

If your level is below 20 ng/mL, supplementation is appropriate. If it's 30+ ng/mL, additional supplementation is unlikely to provide benefit. Don't take high doses without testing.

How do I know if I need a supplement?

The best way to determine if you need a supplement is through blood tests and consultation with a healthcare provider. Consider testing if you have:

  • Unexplained fatigue (iron, B12, vitamin D)
  • Dietary restrictions (B12 for vegans)
  • Pregnancy or planning pregnancy (folic acid)
  • Medications that deplete nutrients (metformin → B12; PPIs → magnesium)
  • Malabsorption conditions

Taking supplements "just in case" without documented deficiency is generally not recommended. It wastes money and may cause harm.

Practical Questions

When should I take my supplements?

Timing depends on the supplement:

  • Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K): With food containing fat for better absorption
  • Iron: On empty stomach if tolerated (vitamin C helps absorption); take 2-4 hours away from medications
  • Calcium: With food; split doses if taking >500mg; separate from iron and thyroid meds
  • Magnesium: Can be taken any time; some take at night for sleep
  • B vitamins: Morning preferred (may cause insomnia if taken late)
  • Probiotics: Usually with or just before a meal

Consistency matters more than precise timing for most supplements.

Are store-brand supplements okay?

Often yes. Store brands from major retailers (Costco/Kirkland, CVS, Walgreens, Target) have generally performed well in independent testing. Reasons:

  • Large retailers can't afford quality scandals
  • They often use the same manufacturers as name brands
  • Many have their own quality testing programs

That said, always look for third-party certification when available. A USP-verified store brand is better than a non-verified name brand.

Can I trust Amazon reviews?

Be very cautious. Amazon has significant problems with supplements:

  • Fake reviews: Many products have purchased or fabricated reviews
  • Commingled inventory: Real and counterfeit products may be mixed together
  • Third-party sellers: Quality control is inconsistent
  • Counterfeit products: Fake supplements are a real problem

If buying on Amazon, verify the seller (prefer "Ships from and sold by Amazon.com"), check for third-party certifications, and be skeptical of products with thousands of 5-star reviews.

How long until I see results?

It depends on what you're treating and your starting point:

  • Vitamin D deficiency: 4-8 weeks to improve blood levels
  • Iron deficiency: 1-2 weeks for energy improvement; 3-6 months to rebuild stores
  • B12 deficiency: Days to weeks for neurological symptoms; months for complete recovery
  • Magnesium for migraines: 1-3 months to see effect

If you're not deficient in something, you likely won't notice any change from supplementing it. "Feeling more energy" from a supplement when you weren't deficient is usually placebo effect.
