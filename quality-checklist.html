Supplement Quality Checklist

Last reviewed: February 2026 · 7 min read

The supplement industry isn't well-regulated. Products may contain more or less than labeled, contaminants, or even dangerous ingredients. Here's how to identify quality products.

⚠️ Why This Matters

Studies have found that up to 1 in 4 supplements don't contain what they claim. Some contain unlisted ingredients, heavy metals, or prescription drugs. Third-party testing is your best protection.

Look for Third-Party Certification

Independent testing organizations verify that supplements contain what's on the label and are free from contaminants. Look for these seals:

✓ USP Verified

U.S. Pharmacopeia — rigorous testing for purity, potency, and manufacturing quality. The gold standard.

✓ NSF Certified

NSF International — tests for contaminants and verifies label claims. "NSF Certified for Sport" is for athletes.

✓ ConsumerLab

Independent testing with published results. You can search their database (subscription required).

✓ Informed Sport

Tests for banned substances in sports. Important for competitive athletes.

The Quality Checklist

✅ Third-Party Tested

Look for USP, NSF, or ConsumerLab seal on the label. If it's not third-party tested, you're trusting the manufacturer completely.

✅ Full Ingredient Disclosure

All ingredients should be listed with amounts. Beware "proprietary blends" that hide individual doses behind a total amount.

✅ Appropriate Doses

Check that doses match amounts shown effective in research. More isn't always better — some nutrients are harmful in excess.

✅ GMP Certified Facility

Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) certification means the facility follows quality control standards. Look for "manufactured in a GMP facility."

✅ Clear Expiration Date

Products should have clear expiration or "best by" dates and lot numbers for traceability.

Red Flags to Avoid

🚩 Disease Claims

Supplements cannot legally claim to "cure," "treat," or "prevent" disease. Products making such claims are violating FDA rules and may be untrustworthy overall.

🚩 "Miracle" or "Breakthrough" Language

Exaggerated marketing claims often signal low-quality products. Real science is usually more measured in its promises.

🚩 Proprietary Blends

When you see "Proprietary Blend 500mg" followed by 10 ingredients, you have no idea how much of each you're getting. Could be mostly filler.

🚩 Too Good to Be True Prices

Quality testing and ingredients cost money. Dramatically cheap products may cut corners on quality or contain less than labeled.

🚩 No Company Contact Info

Reputable companies provide clear contact information and stand behind their products. Anonymous sellers are a red flag.

Specific Concerns by Category

Herbs & Botanicals

Higher contamination risk. Look for:

  • Standardized extracts with specified active compound percentages
  • Testing for heavy metals and pesticides
  • Species verification (some products contain wrong plant species)

Fish Oil/Omega-3s

Check for:

  • IFOS (International Fish Oil Standards) certification
  • Low oxidation levels (shouldn't smell fishy)
  • Specific EPA/DHA amounts, not just "fish oil"

Probiotics

Look for:

  • CFU count "at expiration" not "at manufacture"
  • Specific strain identification (e.g., Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG)
  • Storage requirements (refrigerated vs. shelf-stable)

Where to Research

  • ConsumerLab.com — Independent testing results (subscription)
  • Examine.com — Evidence-based supplement information
  • NIH Office of Dietary Supplements — Fact sheets on individual supplements
  • Labdoor.com — Tests and ranks supplements

💡 When in Doubt

Ask your doctor or pharmacist for brand recommendations. They often know which brands have good reputations and which to avoid. Some hospitals and clinics maintain approved supplement lists.
